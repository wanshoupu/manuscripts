\chapter{Power of sentinels} \label{ch:sentinels}

Sentinels have been in use long before computer science becomes a science.
Then it is found important application in computer programming.
Have you heard of the infamous term---``Elephant in Cairo''?
It explains the working of a sentinel very well.
The term comes from a magazine essay titled ``Pachydermic Personnel Prediction''
~\cite{elephant-in-Cairo:1989}.
Here is an algorithm that computer scientists came up to answer the question ``How to hunt
elephants in Africa?'':
\begin{clist}
    \caption{\label{enum:elephant-hunt-algo-A}
    Modified algorithm for `Hunting elephant in Africa' for proper termination.}
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item Go to Africa.
        \item Start at the Cape of Good Hope.
        \item Work northward in an orderly manner, traversing the continent alternately east and west.
        \item During each traverse pass,
        \begin{enumerate}
            \item Catch each animal seen.
            \item Compare each animal caught to a known elephant.
            \item Stop when a match is detected.
        \end{enumerate}
    \end{enumerate}
\end{clist}
The above algorithm, however, may not terminate at the north end of Africa in the rare
cases there there is no elephant in Africa.
So the hunters may end up drowning in the Mediterranean Sea.
To avoid that from happening, one may add check after each iteration of the inner loop to make
sure that the north end of Africa has not been reached as the modified algorithm in
~\autoref{enum:elephant-hunt-algo-B} does.
\begin{clist}
    \caption{\label{enum:elephant-hunt-algo-B}
    Modified algorithm for `Hunting elephant in Africa' for proper termination.}
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item Go to Africa.
        \item Start at the Cape of Good Hope.
        \item Work northward in an orderly manner, traversing the continent alternately east and west.
        \item During each traverse pass,
        \begin{enumerate}
            \item Catch each animal seen.
            \item Compare each animal caught to a known elephant.
            \item Stop when a match is detected \emph{or north end of Africa is reached}.
        \end{enumerate}
    \end{enumerate}
\end{clist}
But an experienced computer scientist would place a known elephant
in Cairo beforehand and use the algorithm `\autoref{enum:elephant-hunt-algo-A}' as is.
Of course, after the hunt, they would have to inspect the animal they caught and see if it is the
one they placed.
If it is, then that signals the failure of the hunting trip.
Otherwise, they successfully caught an elephant.
This example is just a joke distributed on the Internet.
However it revealed the basic idea behind using sentinels and the many important aspects of how
to use it.

I first came to learn about sentinels and their usage in the book ``Numerical Receipt in C''
~\cite{NumericalRecipes:1992} in the section ``Quicksort''.
I was really amazed by how the authors of the book carefully avoided burdening the
perfance-critical, fast spinning inner loop with the array boundary checks by the use of sentinels
when partitioning the array elements by the pivot element.
Later on in my own software development practice, I made conscious choice when facing similar
problems.
But I didn't stop there.
I went beyond the classical use of sentinels to signal for a graceful termination.
I summarized my encounter with sentinels in the past and listed the reasons I used sentinels as
follows
\begin{enumerate}
    \item\label{itm:sentinel:performance} Avoid costly condition-checking on performance-critical
    loops.
    \item\label{itm:sentinel:loop-and-a-half} Get rid of `Loop-and-a-half' issue
    as their research in Reference ~\cite{Louden-Lambert-2011}.
    \item\label{itm:sentinel:oddball} Avoid the `Oddball solution' smell
    as listed in the `Refactoring' book ~\cite{Fowler1999}
    \item\label{itm:sentinel:ds} Act as a padding boundary for data structures,
    such as linked list, \textit{etc}., to promote uniform operations.
\end{enumerate}
In all these cases, I found that at the root, \emph{sentinels are added to make the problem
more symmetric}.
``What do you mean by `symmetric'?'' you ask dubiously.
Symmetry is the mathematical explanation of aesthetics.
Symmetric objects are visually pleasing to us because it has `order', is familiar to us, and
thus demand less energy to perceive.

On the opposite, asymmetric objects are irregular, anomalous, requires extra effort to process.
So symmetry represents uniformity, uniformity implies saving in resource.
From \code{for}-loops to regular expressions (regex), from HTTP response status
codes to \code{null} pointers, from recursion to dynamic programming, computer science is a
science that is constantly seeking or creating symmetric information to treat things uniformly,
to minimize energy footprint, and to do more with less.
Sentinels is no exception.

When we say ``It's one minute to 7'', we meant the following: first, put in one minute to make it
7 o'clock for more symmetric representation of time and then take away the minute to mean the
true time 6:59.
In the same way, sentinels does the plus and minus operations.
In the opening example `Elephant in Cairo', an elephant was added in the beginning and
deducted at the end.
In doing so we saved in-loop checks for termination as a result of end of Africa being reached.
Sentinels shifts that logic from performance-critical
loop to non-critical section, from inconvenient location to convenient location.
With this option, we can optimize our coding to be more elegant and performant.
In the following sections, we will have a brief look at a few applications of sentinels to
illustrate the fast application opportunities.

\section{String termination sentinels}\label{sec:stringTerminationSentinels}

Despite of controversial opinions\cite{Ritchie:1993,expensive-one-byte:2011}, the
null-terminated strings in C is another excellent example of sentinel use.
Even though saving memory is a major concern when it was originally designed, the
null-terminated string has many programming advantages.
One example is the expression \code{*p == '\0'} which test if the pointer \code{p} points to end
of the string.
Without using null character as a sentinel, it would not be so convenient to perform this test.
Null-terminated strings in other languages has been deprecated largely due to development
of containers and collections.
But the idea of it is still inspiring to us.
The following example will help illustrate this.
The problem statement is as follows ~\footnote{Credit to Leetcode\url{leetcode.com}}:
\begin{quote}
    Given a non-empty string s consisting of English letters and an abbreviation abbr,
    return whether the string matches with the given abbreviation.
    Abbreviation of word is given as replacing certain substring in the word with its length
    printed in decimal integer.
    \emph{Example 1:}
    Given s = "internationalization", abbr = "i12iz4n":
    Return true.
    \emph{Example 2:}
    Given s = "apple", abbr = "a2e":
    Return false.
\end{quote}
\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.3\textwidth]{sentinels/letter-number-section.jpg}
    \caption{\label{fig:letter-number-section} An example of abbreviation for
    word ``pseudoanthropological''.}
\end{figure}
So in other words, the tasks required for the problem is
\begin{enumerate}
    \item\label{itm:breakup.letter.number} to break up the string into alternating
    letter sections $L_{i}$ and number sections $N_{i}$, for $i = 1,2,\dots$
    \item\label{itm:parse.numbers} parse the number sections as integers
    as the number of letters to omit
    \item\label{itm:check.letters} Check if the letter sections, after skipping
    the corresponding number of letters, match corresponding sections in the word.
\end{enumerate}

One thing that is subtle and may slip your mind is that the letter sections and number
sections, while alternating, may have several cases to be considered (see
~\autoref{tab:letter-number-combination} for more information).
\begin{table}[h]
    \begin{center}
        \caption{Possible combinations
        \label{tab:letter-number-combination}}
        \begin{tabular}{|c|c|c|}
            \hline
            starting group & ending group & example \\
            \hline
            letter & letter & `int8al' \\
            number & number & `5nation2' \\
            letter & number & `inter8' \\
            number & letter & `5national' \\
            \hline
        \end{tabular}
    \end{center}
\end{table}

Given so many degrees of freedom, it must be that we have to deal with each case separately?
Actually not.
In the implementation of ~\autoref{lst:ValidWordAbbrev}, we perform all three tasks,
~\autoref{itm:breakup.letter.number}, ~\autoref{itm:parse.numbers},
and ~\autoref{itm:check.letters} in one loop.
It turns out that coded in such a way, the particular beginning group does not require any special
treatment.
But the ending group does regardless.
Namely, when the ending group is number, the last parsed number and the remaining suffix of the
word would be left unexamined in the loop, causing a potential bug.
One would have to complete a one-time cross-examination of the two after the loop as we do in
~\autoref{lst:ValidWordAbbrev}.

\section{Sentinels in Data Structures}\label{sec:sentinelsInDS}

Sentinel nodes have their niched uses in linked data structures such as linked list and tree.
The rationale behind this is explained below.
Linked data structures are fractured memory chunks, scattered all over the address space,
linked together only through connectors.
While they may have significant amount of nodes on boundaries,
algorithms for operations on these data structures are antagonistic to symmetry-breaking boundary
nodes.
Sentinel nodes are nodes that are spared for the purpose of freeing algorithms from having to
deal with boundary conditions.

This deceivingly simple definition of a linked list conceals exceptional conditions

especially in the proximity of the head node.
``What's so exceptional about it?'' you ask.
It means the condition will often catch one off guard while coding simple algorithms such as

When deleting a node from a linked list, one need to consider if the node to be deleted could
possibly be the `head' node.
If so, one need extra effort in order to get the operation done correctly.
In fact deleting node of linked list in C is such a big deal that functions often take
a doubly-indirect pointer \code{Node** header} as argument as shown in the below function signature.
\begin{lstlisting}

\end{lstlisting}
This should not be unfamiliar with most C programmers.

Some elaboration on sentinels.
The working of sentinels has physical corroborations in nature.
The mechanism sentinels work is reminiscent to that of water vapor condensation, for instance.
In order for water vapor to readily condense, cloud condensation nuclei are required.
Otherwise, oversaturation would occur, that is water vapor remaining in vaporous state below ice
point for hours.
You see, even the process of water vapor condensation is made easier with the use of sentinels.
In fact, many catalysts in chemistry and enzymes in biology work with similar mechanism.
In the context of quantum physics, catalysts and enzymes provide an energy barrier
bypass, a detour to the otherwise normal path, for chemical reactions to take place while
their identities remain intact.
In this sense, it is not an exaggeration to call sentinels the `catalysts for programming'.
