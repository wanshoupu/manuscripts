%\section{About this book}\label{sec:aboutThisBook}

When my doctoral research came to a complete stall after $6$ years of hard work, I was convinced
that I had no future as an unachieved physicist.
Digging into my old dreams, I was delighted to find that my passion for coding,
even though dormant throughout the years, is still burning.


Through self-directed, incremental, and prolonged learning, I built up my software development
skills, one at a time.
It took me about a decade to grow from a mere passionate dilettante to an experienced professional.
During the course, I read vast amount of books, articles, blog posts on software engineering topics
such as design patterns and programming paradigms.
I implemented many fundamental algorithms and data structures.
I attempted to solve as many coding problems as I can find.
I put my learning to test in work which I deem as the ultimate touchstone.


But beyond all these which every ambitious software developer would do,
I have an insatiable passion for one other thing---I periodically reflect on my
past code and review my notes.
I would do anything to get to the bottom of everything that bothers me.
I follow my `guts feeling'---I would not give in if `something in there' upsets me.

Many of the problems that I worked on were revisited up to four or five times over the years.
Each time I was able to gain new understandings and my solution shrinks over time.
I try different solutions to solve the same problem;
I ask myself questions ``Why my implementation works or does not work?''

I painstakingly dedicate quality time to think over bugs I made, bugs I fixed,
and bugs I strategically avoided.
I contemplate on new learning and attempt to reconcile it with what my previous experience.
I draw analogies across seemingly different fields.


Have you ever wondered why comparison-based sorting algorithms are unanimously bound
by $O(N \log N)$?
I managed to gain a psychologically satisfying understanding through enduring contemplations.
Starting with specific sorting algorithms,
I related sorting operations as pumping Shannon's information entropy out of the subject of sorting,
similar to pumping heat out of the room with AC\cite{Shannon-mathematical-1948}.
From there further up, I track it back to the field of statistical
mechanics, developed by Ludwig Boltzmann in the 19th century\cite{landau:1980}.
Finally I settled my quest with modeling the time complexity of sorting algorithms by the
statistical entropy of an isolated system with $N$ particles.
By the way, this result is both surprising and expected to me.


My journey of learning and practicing in the field of computer sciences was materialized in
thousands of pages of Evernotes, $40k$
lines of code solving problems that I found online or offline, \textit{e.g.},
\url{https://projecteuler.net}, \url{https://leetcode.com}, and $100k+$ lines of
production code at top-tier tech companies.
They comprises organic programming experiences, home-made development techniques,
and other observed patterns.
Many of them are not talked about elsewhere.

%\section{Why should you this book?}\label{sec:whyThisBook}

This book is a summary of the struggles and the learning, the stumbles and the gains, the dark
quests and the `aha' moments recorded in my software engineering journey over the years.

I thought about sharing them in a book long ago.
But I didn't take this seriously until fairly recently when
I came across a website posting the research report by Forrester where
it cites that the prolonged software engineering talent shortage has turned from lack-of-quantity
to lack-of-quality \footnote{\url{https://go.forrester.com/research/predictions}}.

That report resonated with my own observation as well.
Starting about $5$ years ago,
I started to note that many other developers, colleagues of mine included,
struggled with the same problems that I used to struggle with.
I also realized that codebases that I happened to work with, proprietary and open source alike,
are filled with copy-n-pasted code that could have been avoided with a little shared function,
intricate logic that could have been simplified into a one-liner,
sneaky bugs caused by an escaped variable from its due scope,
mammoth solutions that could have been refactored into a group of lean, single-purposed functions,
and so on.
It is like in a flight you glimpse out of the window and see duct-tape on the wing.
It may be shocking or outrageous to you that our important transactions actually run
on smelly code.
But ugly code has become the norm rather than exception.
In fact I seldom see succinct code as examples in `Programming Pearls'
and its sort\cite{Bentley:1986:PP}.
Browsing through implementations of fairly dense algorithms, it is not rare to see
the `smells' listed in Martin Fowler's seminal book `Refactor'~\cite{Fowler1999}.


Personally, I've worked with graduates and post-graduates from top-tier schools
during design reviews, code reviews, and technical interviews.
Their resumes are full of buzz words.
They have eye-catching GPAs.
They even spent a great deal of time reading books on software development principles, patterns,
guidance, and advice.
But by end of the day when these best practices are to be put into production code, they
needed help.
These plain facts convey the crying signal that there is a gap between the domain of best practices
and the domain how to practice the best practices.


Bad news is that applying best practices in work usually takes years of hands-on experience.
The real-world problems is dauntingly more complex than toy examples used to explain best practices
in books.
Developers would not realize the value of best practices unless they have first-hand empirical
evidence;
they would not understand the applicability boundary without working out a couple of
examples;
they discover ignorance only after being perplexed;
they learn to deal with conflicting needs only after they fit all different `best practices'
in harmony.


The sole focus for this book is to serve as an example on ``how to practice the best
practices'' in software development.
By reproducing the battles I fought and sharing the insights I gained, I hope to
bridge the body of software development best practices with developers
on the industrial battle field.


\section{Who should read this book?} \label{sec:whoshouldread}

I hope readers would find this book to be somewhat expected as well somewhat unexpected.
As Jon Bentley put it ``This sure looks familiar \ldots but I've never seen this
before.''\cite{Bentley:1986:PP}.
This book is intended for professionals who have already had a solid command of
computer science nonetheless who still find application of the software development best practices
in kaleidoscopic coding situations challenging.
It is assumed that readers are comfortable working with one or two of the popular programming
languages such as \python, \java, C and/or C++.
Basic knowledge in algorithm, data structure, and operating system would be a plus but is not
critical to comprehend the key ideas.

\section{What this book is NOT about}\label{sec:thisBookIsNotAbout}

Some disclaimers are needed here to disperse untrue assumptions for this book.
This book is \emph{not} a programming primer at least not systematically.
Nor does it teach you a specific programming language.

%The focus of this writing is to help beginning and intermediate developers to advance their
%coding skills to next level.
%The techniques discussed here are practical in production coding environment.
%But they are not necessarily helpful during interview settings.
%Therefore this book may be an overkill for interview prep.

This book is not about recipe or cookbook for any specific framework or system.
Rather, it's a general guideline for development with all frameworks and systems.

%Last but not least, I am not proposing new rules or new principles.
%Many of the principles, programming patterns already exists.
%What I attempt here is to show you how to turn these principles to correct and elegant code.


Also this book does not offer problem-solving strategies or
quick-n-dirty programming `tips' designed for coding interviews.
Nevertheless, if absorbed into one's coding habit, the techniques in this book \emph{do} help
with build up one's code sense and instinct which, in turn, helps with technical interviews
in the long run.


Last but not least, this book is not a typical preparation material for coding interviews.
Coding interview emphasizes primarily on `Get it done'.
But the focus of this book is to `Get it done right'.
Readers are encouraged to strive for long-term investment of improving their programming skills.
Also many of the techniques presented in this book such as ~\fullref{ch:organizeYourIfElse} or
~\fullref{ch:reusableFunctions} requires more time than allowed in an interview setting.
So don't be bound by them in an interview setting.
Nevertheless, those who are preparing for technical interviews will benefit from this book,
if this book may have formative inspirations over them.

Finally, I hope the reader would take this book as a seed rather than food, as an inspiration for
life-long endeavors rather than sharing of achievement for applause.
