    \begin{abstract}
        We present a quantum system software framework that bridges the gap between physics modeling and quantum software development.
        At the core of this framework is a versatile quantum compiler capable of decomposing arbitrary Hamiltonians into quantum circuits, represented using a platform-independent B-Tree intermediate language (IL).
        The B-Tree structure provides essential lineage information for circuit gate traceability and code verification.
        The IL offers a universal, hardware-agnostic representation that enables flexible translation and optimization across diverse quantum hardware backends.
        We demonstrate implementations that render the IL into executable quantum circuits targeting platforms such as Qiskit and Cirq.
        Additionally, we provide a transpiler that converts the IL into OpenQASM for broader compatibility.
    \end{abstract}

    \section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

    Quantum simulation offer significant advantages for applications in chemistry, pharmaceuticals, and biological processes.
    Given an input Hamiltonian $\hat{H}$, the quantum dynamics are fully prescribed by the time-dependent evolution operator
    \begin{equation}
        \label{eq:hamiltonian}
        \hat{U}\left( t \right) = e^{-i\hat{H}t/\hbar}
    \end{equation}
    In Schrödinger picture, for example, the time-dependent state ket of the system is given as
    \begin{equation}
        \label{eq:sysstate}
        \left\| \psi\left( t \right) \right> = \hat{U}\left( t \right)\left\| \psi_{0} \right>
    \end{equation}
    where $\left\| \psi_{0} \right>$ is the initial ket of an arbitrary system state.
    Whereas, most observable operators remain constant unless they are explicitly time-dependent.
    Equivalently in Heisenberg picture, the dynamics may be formulated as
    \begin{equation}
        \label{eq:heisenberg}
        \hat{A} \left( t \right) = \hat{U}^{\dagger} \left( t \right) \hat{A}_{0} \hat{U} \left( t \right)
    \end{equation}
    where $\hat{A}_{0}$ is the initial operator for an arbitrary observable.
    Whereas, the state kets remain constants.
    By choosing a suitable basis, the evolution operator, $\hat{U}\left( t \right)$, may be represented as a unitary matrix $U$.

    Despite its potential in quantum simulation, developing quantum computing software remains highly challenging,
    requiring expertise in physics, mathematics, and software engineering.
    This steep learning curve limits access for many users and professionals who could benefit from quantum computing.
    Existing quantum transpilers are often platform-specific, implemented in an ad hoc manner,
    and provide limited control over compilation granularity.


    To address these challenges, we developed \texttt{qompiler}, a cross-platform quantum circuit synthesizer
    designed for traceability, verifiability, and configurable gate control.
    \texttt{Qompiler} decomposes time slices of evolution operator, $U$, into circuits using a multi-stage framework,
    including two-level, swap, control, Euler angle decompositions, and Solovay-Kitaev approximations.
    The intermediate representation (IL) is structured as a B-Tree, enabling gate-level lineage and platform independence,
    and it supports major frameworks such as Qiskit, Cirq, and OpenQASM~\cite{cirq2024,qiskit2023,openqasm2,openqasm3}.
    Notably, the compiler allows fine-grained gate selection to match native hardware capabilities—for example,
    using Pauli gates on superconducting hardware or rotation gates on trapped-ion devices—facilitating
    hardware-aware optimization and broad applicability.

    \end{itemize}

        The computer icon represents user input.

    Here we provide an overview of the high-level design.
    The system is organized into the following functional parts:
    ``Compiler configuration'', ``Matrix decomposition'', ``Optimization'', ``Circuit rendering''.
    This is illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:impl}.
    We will briefly describe each of these steps here, while more detailed discussions of selected components
    are presented in subsequent sections.


    A key feature of \texttt{qompiler} is its configurable compilation granularity,
    allowing users to tailor the resulting circuit to the gate sets best suited for the target hardware.
    For instance, superconducting quantum hardware often implements Pauli gates ($X$, $Y$, $Z$) virtually,
    offering zero-error and zero-latency execution;
    in this case, the compiler can be configured to prioritize Pauli gates.
    Conversely, trapped-ion platforms such as IonQ natively support arbitrary single-qubit rotations,
    including $R_x(\phi)$, $R_y(\phi)$, and $R_z(\phi)$, and the compiler can be adapted accordingly to produce rotation-based circuits.
    This hardware-aware configurability enables efficient, optimized circuit generation across diverse quantum backends
    while maintaining traceability and cross-platform compatibility.


    The compiler configuration is the sole responsibility of \texttt{ConfigManager}.
    \texttt{ConfigManager} can read configuration inputs from several sources:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Default settings JSON file,
        \item Custom overrides JSON file,
        \item Command line settings.
    \end{itemize}
    Rich controls of the compilation processes are made possible through the sophisticated configuration system,
    offering control over almost all aspects of the compilation process,
    including but not limited to the granularity control, target platform, error tolerances,
    quantum device space allocation, optimization level, etc.

    Once all configuration sources are loaded and combined,
    the final settings are used to create a singleton instance of \texttt{QFactory},
    which is a creational design pattern, the factory pattern ~\cite{Gamma1994DesignPattern}.
    From the \texttt{QFactory}, other compilation constructs are created as
    needed---\texttt{Qompiler}, \texttt{QRenderer}, \texttt{QDevice}, \texttt{QConfig}, \texttt{Optimizers}, etc.


    Now that all the needed constructs are in place, \texttt{Qompiler} can start to take orders---\( U\left(t\right) \) as input and
    compile it into gate-level quantum code.
    The compilation process comprises several key steps, including two-level decomposition (2L), swap gate decomposition (CX),
    control gate decomposition (CTRL), Euler rotation decomposition (EA),
    and the Solovay-Kitaev approximation (SK)~\cite{nielsen2010q,dawson2005sk}.
    This is done recursively by the \texttt{Qompiler} class.
    We will spend the entire Section~\ref{sec:decomp} to discuss this as well as future development.


    The final compiled code (quantum circuit) is represented in a B-Tree data structure,
    an intermediate language (IL) designed specifically for the task.
    IL encodes gate-level lineage via parent-child relationships among tree nodes, thereby providing robust traceability
    and verifiability, which will be discussed in Section~\ref{sec:traceability-and-verifiability}.


    Nearing the end of the compilation process are the optimization passes
    where multiple passes may be taken to simplify the circuit output,
    including gate annihilation, reduction, and consolidation.
    Think of the decomposition processes as `push`, whereas the optimization processes as `pull`.
    Through the optimization processes, we `pull back` some of the decomposition actions.
    By combining the actions in both the `push` and `pull` processes, we aim to find an optimal compilation result
    that respects the native constraints of the targeted quantum hardware.
    This will be the topic for Section~\ref{sec:optimization}.


    Once the compilation process is finished, the end result is binary code written to disk as `.qco' files.
    Finally, the interpretation stage.
    The circuit interpretation and rendering part is in the curation of \texttt{QRenderer} and \texttt{CircuitBuilder}.
    The compiler’s intermediate language (IL) is platform-independent and can be interpreted and rendered
    into executable circuits for specific quantum platforms.
    The IL can also be translated into other quantum software standards, such as OpenQASM, for broader compatibility~\cite{openqasm2,openqasm3}.
    Note that the compilation is done once, but the circuit interpreting and rendering may be done any number of times,
    for any supported platforms.
    This stage is designed to be highly adaptable and open to new platforms.
    Currently supported quantum computing platforms include Qiskit and Cirq.
    Support for other platforms can be added easily by writing a circuit builder extending the base class \texttt{CircuitBuilder}.
    We will visit this in Section~\ref{sec:rendering}.

    In this section, we will lay the foundation of terminologies for discussions in this article.
    We will not go over all the elements for quantum simulation.
    Instead, we will focus on a few key elements of quantum circuit and refer readers to references such as~\cite{nielsen2010q}
    for the basics.
    Representations of unitary matrices and quantum gates, especially, controlled quantum gates,
    are the central part of the compiler design.
    A unitary matrix refers to a multidimensional unitary matrix of complex type.
    It captures the dynamics of a specific quantum system.
    It usually has no reference to any specific qubit for the simulation.
    A quantum gate, on the other hand, is a predefined unit of quantum operation acting on a specific domain of qubits.
    We call the specific domain of qubits a ``quantum space'' and refer to it as \texttt{qspace} in this article.
    The compilation process, which will be discussed in another section in more details,
    transfers the encoded dynamics of the unitary matrix to that of a network of quantum gates.

    \subsection{Unitary Matrices}\label{subsec:unitary-matrices}

    We created \texttt{UnitaryM} to represent unitary matrices.
    Given the sparse nature of the evolution operator, $\hat{U}\left( t \right)$,
    we designed \texttt{UnitaryM} readily accommodate sparsity and still be efficient.
    For example, for a $10$-qubit Hamiltonian system, it's rarely the case that all $10$ qubits interact with one another.
    More often, qubits next to one another interact.
    Reflected in the evolution operator, these correspond to the off-diagonal elements.


    Accordingly, we only store the rows and columns with non-identity diagonal elements and/or
    with non-zero off-diagonal elements.
    We call the indices of these rows and columns the ``core-indices''
    and the submatrix corresponding to these rows and columns the ``core-matrix''.
    We implemented the \texttt{matmul} method for matrix multiplication operations between \texttt{UnitaryM}'s.
    The class method \texttt{deflate} converts a NumPy array ($2$D) into \texttt{UnitaryM}.
    The method \texttt{inflate} (and \texttt{numpy.array}) does the opposite---returns
    the matrix in the format of \texttt{numpy} $2$D array.

    As stated earlier, what distinguish a quantum gate from a unitary operator is
    that a quantum gate is allocated with a domain, a \texttt{qspace}.
    Each qubit in a \texttt{qspace} can assume one of three roles:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \texttt{TARGET}, whose quantum state may be altered.
        \item \texttt{CONTROL}, whose quantum state undergoes no change.
        It comes with two subtypes \texttt{CONTROL0} with activation value of zero ($0$) and \texttt{CONTROL1} with activation value of one ($1$).
        \item \texttt{IDLER}, who does not participate in the gate operation at all.
    \end{itemize}
    For ease of reference, we introduce `granularity' as a classification of quantum gates
    which is roughly characterized by the following:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item The number of involved qubits
        \item The interactions among the qubits
        \item The roles played by each qubit
    \end{itemize}

    In Table~\ref{tab:terminology}, we listed all the major types of quantum gates.
    For all the types of quantum gates, we designed one class `\texttt{CtrlGate}'---single qubit or
    multi-qubit, single-target or multi-target, with or without control qubits.

    The \texttt{CtrlGate} can be built from Pauli operators, $X$, $Y$, $Z$, Hadamard operator $H$, phase operator, $S$, $T$,
    rotational matrices, $R_x(\phi)$, $R_y(\phi)$, and $R_z(\phi)$, or arbitrary unitary matrices.
    The \texttt{CtrlGate} class is also the workhorse of most operations involving controlled gates:
    matrix multiplications, `\texttt{matmul}', expanding into extra Hilbert space, qubit sorting,
    trace operation, and direct conversion of \texttt{UnitaryM} into \texttt{CtrlGate}.


    As keen readers have noticed, \texttt{IDLER} qubits are degrees of freedom that can be added into
    or taken away from the formulation of \texttt{CtrlGate}.
    Expansion into extra Hilbert space is an important operation
    when multiplying multi-qubit operators spanning different qubit spaces.
    Adding extra qubits to \texttt{CtrlGate} is implemented by the method \texttt{expand}.
    By performing successive Kronecker product operations, one can inject idler qubits in between the original qubit spaces.


    Another operation, useful especially for circuit verification, is the \texttt{trace} operation.
    Because of the use of ancilla qubits, we sometimes want to eliminate these qubits from the multi-qubit gates
    and compare the remaining gate reproduce the input.
    Without the trace operation, verification of the compilation process would be challenging.
    Class diagrams for the two classes are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:um-and-cgate}.


    \section{Decompositions}\label{sec:decomp}

    The compilation process encodes the dynamics into a network of quantum gates, allocates and assigns qubits to each gate,
    and by the latter, we attempt to attain the overall quantum dynamics precisely or approximately.
    In this section, we go over the decomposition process of the \texttt{qompiler}.


    Starting from an arbitrary $n \times n$-dimensional unitary operator,
    the encoded quantum formulation undergoes a number of decomposition processes.
%    TODO
    From an arbitrary $n \times n$-dimensional unitary operator to single-qubit gates and/or \texttt{XNOT} two-qubit interaction gates,
    Given a unitary operator as input, the decomposition process breaks it down into a sequence of gates of the desirable type
    so that the matrix product of the latter recovers the former.
    In this section, we detail some aspects of the decomposition processes used by \texttt{qompiler}, including
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \texttt{mat2l\_decompose}: decompose an arbitrary $n \times n$ unitary matrix into two-level unitary matrices.
        \item \texttt{cnot\_decompose}: decompose a two-level unitary matrix into single-target operators in universal gates.
        \item \texttt{ctrl\_decompose}: given a single-qubit \texttt{CtrlGate}, decompose its control sequences to no more than a certain length, usually one or two qubits.
        \item \texttt{euler\_decompose}: given a controlled unitary matrix, decompose it into Euler rotation operators and optionally a phase shift.
        \item \texttt{cliffordt\_decompose}: given a standard gate, decompose it into \texttt{Clifford} + \texttt{T} gates.
    \end{itemize}

    These decomposition methods are orchestrated by the class \texttt{Qompiler}.
    \texttt{QDevice} is used for allocating qubits and ancilla qubits.

    The entry point is \texttt{Unitary}.
    If the configuration specifies a granularity of \texttt{MULTI\_TARGET},
    the decomposition terminates with multi-target operators.
    Otherwise, the unitary operator is sequentially decomposed into two-level unitary operators,
    single-qubit gates, and control-pruned gates.
    A granularity probe of type \texttt{PRINCIPAL} is then performed to determine
    whether the target granularity has been reached.
    If so, the output consists of principal rotational operators.
    If not, the decomposition continues into either Clifford+T gate sets or user-defined universal gate sets.

    \section{Optimizers}\label{sec:optimization}
    \texttt{Qompiler} compiles the input unitary operator into a sequence of target quantum gates
    through successive decomposition processes, analogous to a digestion process.
    This often produces consecutive gates that annihilate one another or gates with smaller-than-desired granularity.
    To compensate for this, we included the optimization passes act as a synthesis process to simplify the output circuit,
    combining multiple operators into fewer, more efficient gates.

    The optimizer class follows the sliding window design.

    \section{Traceability and Verifiability}\label{sec:traceability-and-verifiability}

    One of the key features of the current implementation of \texttt{qompiler} is
    its built-in support for traceability and verifiability.
    When applying quantum computing to real-world problems, such as quantum chemistry,
    the size of the quantum circuit can become very large.
    A quantum program for simulating a simple molecule like LiH in the STO-3G basis
    may require approximately $1000$ gates~\cite{Hong_2022}.
    Simulation programs for moderately large molecules can consist of millions~\cite{poulin2014,google_science_2020}.
    Keeping track of all these gates and ensuring correctness is challenging.
    \texttt{Qompiler} is designed with traceability and verifiability in mind from the outset.
    We organize the compiled code as a B-Tree data structure in the intermediate language.
%    TODO
    To that end, we created \texttt{ByteCode} to represent the tree node.

    \begin{figure}[tbhp]
        \centering
        \includegraphics[width=0.6\linewidth]{btree}
        \caption{The end result as a B-Tree data structure.
        The B-Tree is layered, starting from the input unitary matrix at the root,
            followed by intermediate layers two-level unitary, single-qubit, control-pruned, and finally universal gates
            (including Clifford+T gates) as the leaves.
        }
        \label{fig:btree}
    \end{figure}

    The resulting IL is represented as a B-Tree data structure and stored in \textit{.qco} files.
    In the B-Tree structure, parent nodes generally correspond to coarser-grained operators,
    while child nodes represent the decomposed operators whose product recovers the parent node, e.g.,
    \begin{equation}
        \label{eq:parent-child-relation}
        \hat{P} = \prod_{i=1}^{n} \hat{C}_{i}.
    \end{equation}
    In general, the nodes of a B-Tree are layered hierarchically.
    See Fig.~\ref{fig:btree} for details.

    Traceability is encoded in the parent-child relationships.
    Each gate in the final program can be traced back to the specific operation that produced it.
    The built-in optimizers applied in later stages of compilation also record tracing information for their operations
    and preserve the original lineage.

    Overall, debugging the quantum circuit is made easier in \texttt{qompiler}
    thanks to the traceability and verifiability integral to the design.

    \section{Limitations}\label{sec:limitations}
    The entire compiler project was implemented in Python with numerical libraries such as NumPy and SciPy.
    Consequently, the underlying numerical errors limit the precision of the compiled quantum circuit.
    However, when the Solovay--Kitaev approximation is employed,
    the dominant source of error is the residual approximation error itself.

    As the size of the input increases, practical system limitations arise.
    The program becomes computationally intensive for large unitary matrices.
    The memory footprint may also become a bottleneck once the number of qubits reaches a certain threshold.
    It is expected that the pressure on computational resources can be alleviated through distributed computation.


    \section{Conclusion and Future Work}\label{sec:conclusion-and-future-work}
    We presented a configurable, cross-platform quantum compiler, \texttt{Qompiler}, with built-in gate traceability.
    Initial benchmarks confirm the correctness and consistency of \texttt{Qompiler}'s
    output across supported platforms, including Qiskit and Cirq.
    Future work will focus on distributed computation and parallelization to enable the
    compilation of large input matrices and to improve overall efficiency.
    We plan to extend compatibility to additional frameworks, such as AWS Braket and PennyLane,
    with the broader goal of streamlining quantum algorithm development across the heterogeneous quantum
    computing landscape~\cite{braket-zenodo,pennylane}.
    We are also considering implementing sophisticated qubit allocation algorithms
    to adapt to given topologies and interaction strategies such as $1$D linear chain,
    $2$D grid, star, all-to-all, etc.
    Furthermore, we aim to incorporate quantum error correction techniques to enhance the reliability of synthesized circuits.
    We welcome collaboration and feedback from the quantum software community.
