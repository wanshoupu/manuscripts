\chapter{Organize your Conditional Statements}\label{ch:organizeYourIfElse}

Conditional is a control switching mechanism to alter code execution path,
conditioned by boolean expressions.
As a control flow, conditional is one of the fundamental building blocks
of almost all programming languages.
Undoubtedly, many references---textbooks, research articles, blog posts---have already covered this
comprehensive discussions
\footnote{\url{https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conditional_(computer_programming)}}.
It may seem strange that I am spending a chapter on such a `well-known' topic here and now.
But based on my years of working experience in several top-tier tech companies,
haphazard suboptimal solutions with convoluted and lengthy logic make their way to
production codebase and becomes the
core for millions of businesses.
The crooked design, the obscure the logic later pose as road-block and hazard on the way of
code refactor and reuse.
This shows that just because the topic is basic and `well-known' does not mean that folks can
or will do it right.
%But it is seldom the case that the first version of implementation of any non-trivial logic, if
%correct at all, is optimal.
``As long as it works, why does one bother to simplify the logic?'', some may argue.
My answer is ``Suboptimal, obscure logic fails the primary purpose of coding---communication.''
Worse, it often results in redundant/duplicate code which lays outrageous burden on code
maintenance.
I'd like to quote Steve McConnell
\begin{quote}
    ``..the primary audience [of computer programs] is made up of humans,
    not computers\cite{Mcconnell.2004}.''
\end{quote}
Martin Fowler put it even more straightforward
\begin{quote}
    ``Any fool can write code that a computer can understand.
    Good programmers write code that humans can understand\cite{Fowler1999}.''
\end{quote}

Twisted code and intertwined logic

I don't blame the author of twisted code and intertwined logic, which
are often the victorious output of a fierce battle fought by gallant developers.
They has all my respect.
But I criticize anyone who intends half-cooked work for final product in defiance of the
production standard.
Unequivocally I advocate that developers finish the other half of the work
---polish the logic, simplify the conditional statements, craft production-quality solutions.
Developers finish walking the last mile, solidify your otherwise hard-won victory.

With simple techniques and due diligence, producing beautiful, succinct solution
with initially unrefined code is possible.
The rest of this chapter is dedicated to this.

Comparing with ~\autoref{lst:nestedBranching} the conditional statements are now streamlined and
easier to understand.
But still it has code duplication as both \code{statement2} and \code{statement3} are repeated.
So I did a back-of-envelop calculation---
I obtained the needed conditions in order for \code{statement1}, \code{statement2} and
\code{statement3} to be executed, respectively.

The result is shown in ~\autoref{tbl:breakdown-of-conditions}.
With this table, it is clear that the key to simplify the logic lies in the conditions for the
execution of \code{statement2}.
Let us do some Boolean algebra.
Let $c$ be the condition for \code{statement2} to be executed and
\begin{align*}
    a &= \text{\code{test1}}, \\
    b &= \text{\code{test2}}.
\end{align*}
Then
\begin{align}
    c &= a \land \lnot b \lor \lnot a  \nonumber \\
    &= a \land \lnot b \lor (\lnot a \land \lnot b \lor \lnot a \nonumber) & \text{// absorption law
    }\\
    &= (a \land \lnot b \lor \lnot a \land \lnot b) \lor \lnot a \nonumber & \text{// associativity
    law}\\
    &= \lnot b \lor \lnot a & \text{// distributivity law} \nonumber \\
    &=\lnot (a \land b) & \text{// De Morgan's law} \label{eq:boolean-expression-simplify}
\end{align}

~\autoref{eq:boolean-expression-simplify} shows that the condition of execution for
\code{statement2} is \code{!(test1 && test2)} and this happens to fit as the
\code{else}-block of the conditional statement \code{if (test1 && test2)..}.
With this hard-earned wit, it now becomes straightforward to come up with yet an even simpler
implementation such as ~\autoref{lst:final-simplified-conditional-statement}.
This completes the whole logic simplification process.

Note that there are other ways to achieve the same goal.
One can also use \emph{truth tables} to simplify logic.
Let's see how this works.
\begin{table}[h]
    \begin{center}
        \caption{True value table for \code{test1} and \code{test2}.
        \label{tbl:true-value-table}}
        \begin{tabular}{|c|c|c|}
            \hline
            \code{test1} & \code{test2} & true expr \& execution\\
            \hline
            \code{false} & \code{false} & \code{!test1} $\rightarrow$ \code{statement2} \\
            \code{false} & \code{true} & \code{!test1} $\rightarrow$ \code{statement2} \\
            \code{true} & \code{false} & \code{test1 \&\& !test2}
            $\rightarrow$ \code{statement2} \\
            & & \code{test1=true} $\rightarrow$ \code{statement3} \\
            \code{true} & \code{true} & \code{test1 \&\& test2} $\rightarrow$
            \code{statement1} \\
            & & \code{test1} $\rightarrow$ \code{statement3} \\
            \hline
        \end{tabular}
    \end{center}
\end{table}
I have constructed a truth table with \code{test1} and \code{test2} as the variables
(~\autoref{tbl:true-value-table}).
Since there are two conditional variables, there are a total four rows in the table.
Additionally, we added the third column to shows what \code{true} boolean expression leads
the execution of which statement.
Summarizing the table entries, we arrive at the following conclusions
\begin{enumerate}[label={\roman*:}]
    \item \code{statement1} is executed \emph{iff} both \code{test1} and \code{test2}
    are \code{true};
    \item \code{statement2} is executed \emph{iff} not both \code{test1} and \code{test2} are
    \code{true};
    \item \code{statement3} is executed \emph{iff} \code{test1} is \code{true}, regardless of
    \code{test2}.
\end{enumerate}
With all the mental twists untied, implementation of something like
~\autoref{lst:final-simplified-conditional-statement} naturally follows.

Thus far we have presented two processes for logic simplification.
One of them en routes on the necessary and sufficient conditions for the execution of certain
statements and use that to simplify the conditional expressions.
The other lists all the possible branches in a true table and looks for simple ways to combine
them into as few conditional expressions as possible.
In practice, you are encouraged to try both and form an intuition for each process.
You will find each of them has an edge for certain circumstances.

\section{Short-circuit evaluation}
\label{sec:short-circuit-eval}

Short-circuit evaluation (SCE) refers to the omission of evaluation of certain operand(s)
when the overall result of the boolean expression would not be changed by the result of their
evaluation(s).
SCE is supported by the C-family (also the mainstream) programming languages such as C, C++, \java,
\python and others.
SCE is an interesting feature for better or for worse.
On the one hand, unprepared developers may be caught by surprise and
inadvertent usage may results in unexpected results.
On the other it is the pivotal foundation for constructing extremely elegant logical statements.
We will use a couple of examples to illustrate skills about SCE programming.

Consider the following requirement:
\begin{quote}
    ``Implement a method, \code{next}, to fetch the next item from a source
    which is irrelevant to this problem and
    a method \code{procure} has been made available to fetch an element from the source and
    stored it in a member variable \code{Optional<Object> cache} as
    \lstinputlisting{if-else/getNext_procure.java}
    The cache shall be cleared with each successful invocation of method \code{next}.
    ''
\end{quote}
This is a subtask extracted from a class design specification.
Presumably, the algorithm to solve this problem could be
\begin{quote}
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item Check \code{cache} to see if it is empty, if so
        \begin{enumerate}
            \item Invoke \code{procure} to attempt to populate \code{cache}
            \item If attempt of procuring an item is unsuccessful, throw ``No element is left''
            exception
        \end{enumerate}
        \item Get the item from cache and clear the cache
        \item Return the item
    \end{enumerate}
\end{quote}

Let's see how we can implement the algorithm in \java in the absence of SCE mechanism.
\lstinputlisting[caption={Implementation of \code{next} in the absence of SCE mechanism},
label={lst:next-without-sce}]
{if-else/getNext.java}
That is not bad, right?
The code seems pretty simple by itself.
But in reality \java supports SCE and with it one can readily implement
the following
\lstinputlisting[caption={Implementation of \code{next} method taking advantage of SCE in\java.},
label={lst:next-sce}]
{if-else/getNext_shortCircuit.java}
How does ~\autoref{lst:next-sce} work?
When \code{cache} \emph{is absent}, regardless of the return value of the method
\code{procure}, the overall value of the conditional expression will be \code{false}.
By SCE mechanism, the compiler will \emph{omit} the evaluation of the rest operands in the
conditional expression.
So the overall effect is when \code{cache} is absent, the method \code{procure} will \emph{not}
be invoked.
The entire \code{if}-statement is skipped and the item in \code{cache} is all the business.
When \code{cache} is present, the evaluation process is back to normal:
the conditional expression continues with the evaluation of
the second part, which in turn, invokes \code{procure} and everything else ensues after that.

Comparing ~\autoref{lst:next-sce} with ~\autoref{lst:next-without-sce}, we saw the former is an
elegant, succinct implementation of otherwise messy code, thanks to SCE mechanism.

Through this example, we learn the the important aspects for SCE programming.
First of all, order of operands matters, \textit{e.i.}, \code{a && b} is not always equivalent to
\code{b && a} if the evaluation of one or both of them have side effects.
Secondly, if one consciously seeks to take advantage of SCE mechanism to avoid unnecessary
evaluate of some statements under certain circumstances, wrap them in a function with
compact signature.
To that end, one needs to consciously modularize one's design, creating reusable, meaningful
single-responsible subroutines to be invoked at higher level.
For example one needs to create functions \code{procure} with a well-defined return value of
boolean type that indicates
if procurement of an item is successful or not for use in the short-circuit boolean
expression in ~\autoref{lst:next-sce}.
One need to be aware of characteristics such as idempotence and mutability of the
function invocation and so on.
We will talk more about function modularity design in ~\fullref{ch:reusableFunctions}


\section{Case-stack}\label{sec:case-stack}

Hopefully the sequence of consecutive conditional statements as shown in
~\autoref{lst:simpleBranching} is not unfamiliar to reader.
It begins with \code{if} clause, followed by zero, one, or more of
\code{else if} blocks (\code{elif} for \python),
and optionally concludes with an \code{else} block.
In fact I see this again and again from proprietary repositories to open source codebase.
For the sake of argument, we refer to such a construct as `case-stack'.
Case-stack is an important pattern for coding.
In combination with loop constructs or used within recursive functions,
case-stack is an elegant programming pattern for implementing complicated logic.
You can see its applications throughout this book.
Please refer to examples in ~\fullref{ch:loops}, ~\fullref{ch:recursion}, and the chapters in
~\fullref{prt:case-studies} for more discussion.

Unfortunately, little is mentioned about this patterns in popular programming references.
Beginning and intermediate developers often stumble upon this little programming gem haphazardly,
not to mention mastering the best practice of it.
We so hope that in this section some of the important aspects of case-stack be given a fair
coverage to promote the best practices around it.

%TODO
When faced with multi-branch switching,
a common way of implementation uses nested \code{if..else} statements such as the example given at
the beginning of this chapter ~\autoref{lst:nestedBranching}.
As switching fanout grows, the nesting level goes deeper, indentation grows messier, and
the logic quickly becomes unwieldy.
As an alternative for better readability, one can organize multi-branch switching using a
case-stack as ~\autoref{lst:simpleBranching}.
For some special cases, \code{switch}-statement may be used equally well, if supported by the
programming language.
For simplicity, we limit our discussion to \code{if..else}-statement here.

The execution model of a case-stack is like walking up a staircase---the program
successively evaluates the conditional expression one at a time and builds up the cases.
The conditional expression of the leading \code{if}-block is the first step of check.
If it is evaluated to \code{true}, then the \code{if}-block is executed
and the rest of the stack is skipped.
Otherwise, the next conditional expression is evaluated
and so on until the end of the case-stack.
Note that in the presence of \code{return} and \code{break},
the case-stack may be simplified with the removal of the \code{else} keyword.
For examples, take a look at ~\autoref{lst:if-stack-equals}, ~\autoref{lst:if-stack-lca},
and ~\fullref{lst:matrix-spiral-term} in ~\fullref{ch:recursion}.

First property of a case-stack is the strict order of conditional statements which cannot be
altered once completed.
The order of conditional statements takes careful design for succinct code,
optimal performance or both.
The second property is the cumulative effect of conditional expressions.
We are going to talk these properties below.

When taken apart, the effect of tests of a case-stack
is cumulative---a later case combines all previous conditional expressions up to its own.
This is illustrated by the example of ~\autoref{tab:cumulative-tests-case-stack}.
Note that unlike the strict ordering of the conditional statements in the left column,
the three \code{if}-blocks in the right column are not logically coupled and

When debugging a case-stack for logic fallacies and unfounded assumptions, for example,
one needs to keep this cumulative effect in mind: all the assumptions about the

As a real-world example to demonstrate the effectiveness of case-stack in handling
multi-branching logic,
let's look at a typical \code{equals} method in a subclass that overrides
the default implementation in \java.
As shown in ~\autoref{lst:if-stack-equals},
The first test we do is the nullity of the argument.
Only if it is not null do we move on to the second test---if it's of type \code{MyClass}.
Only if it is not null and is of type \code{MyClass} do we cast it to type \code{MyClass}
and perform pairwise equality tests between member variables of
\code{this} instance and \code{that} instance.
Once again, you see how the program progresses up a `staircases' step-by-step.

\begin{figure}[htb]
    \lstinputlisting[label={lst:if-stack-lca},
    caption={An implementation of finding lowest common ancestor in a binary search tree.}]
    {if-else/lca.java}
\end{figure}
Earlier we pointed out the strict ordering property of case-stack.
To illustrate how the order of tests affects the construct of case-stack,
let's consider the problem:
\begin{quote}
    ``determining the lowest common ancestor of two values in a binary search tree.''
\end{quote}
By the nature of binary search tree and the problem requirements, the number of `special' cases
is large.
If not designed properly, the code of handling all the cases can blow up like a nightmare.
However, with a properly designed case-stack as we are going to show next, a couple of lines of
code can handle the myriad possibilities like a breeze.
Take a look at ~\autoref{lst:if-stack-lca}.
The cases are built from ground up like this:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Test nullity;
    \item Test for left branching;
    \item Test for right branching;
    \item Test for existence of keys;
    \item Final case;
\end{enumerate}
Note later statements are built on the assertions of previous tests---\textit{e.g.}, test for
left/right branching requires the non-null attributes of root otherwise the code will throw
\code{NullPointerException}.
Also note that as the cases are being built up, the solution space is also being halved
and ruled out much like that of a binary search.
Some source states that when doing tests to rule out cases in settings such as a case-stack, start
with the simplest one.
This observation is quite superficial.
The real criterion is to do the test that contains most entropy, the test that is most
informative, the test that is hinged with most stake first.
That way, the maximal solution space can be chopped off at the earliest possible time.
Generally speaking, building up the cases of an case-stack is ultimately empirical.
Even the experienced developers may have to make a couple of attempts to get the optimal design.
So go ahead and try this out this in your own practice and sharpen your instinct about it.

\section{Conditional statements in a loop}\label{sec:branchingInLoop}

Conditional statements in a loop is one of the most important application.
With the keywords \code{break} and \code{continue}, conditional statements
have more moving parts, therefore logic simplification in a loop is even more challenging.
On the other hand, this also means skilled developers have new weapons to wield when aiming at
simple, succinct logic.

Practical programming tips for loops will be more fully discussed ~\autoref{ch:loops}.
Many of the topics overlap between here and there,
so we are going to be brief here and with emphasis on conditional constructs.
For fuller discussions, we direct readers there.

For a really good example of conditional application in a loop,
the ~\fullref{lst:silhouette-skyline} presented in ~\fullref{ch:loop-thinning}
would make an excellent example.

Like a complex protein molecule, the conditional statements are assembled into a case-stack in
resemblance of hierarchical structures and together they perform the processing in a stateful loop.
