    \begin{abstract}
        We present a quantum system software framework that bridges the gap between physics modeling and quantum software development.
        At the core of this framework is a versatile quantum compiler capable of decomposing arbitrary Hamiltonians into quantum circuits, represented using a platform-independent B-Tree intermediate language (IL).
        The B-Tree structure provides essential lineage information for circuit gate traceability and code verification.
        The IL offers a universal, hardware-agnostic representation that enables flexible translation and optimization across diverse quantum hardware backends.
        We demonstrate implementations that render the IL into executable quantum circuits targeting platforms such as Qiskit and Cirq.
        Additionally, we provide a transpiler that converts the IL into OpenQASM for broader compatibility.
    \end{abstract}

    \section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

    Quantum simulation offers exponential advantages for applications in chemistry, pharmaceuticals, and biological processes.
    Despite its potential, developing quantum software remains highly challenging, demanding deep expertise in physics, mathematics, and software engineering.
    This steep learning curve limits access for many users and professionals who could potentially benefit.
    Additionally, existing quantum transpilers are often platform-specific, done in ad hoc, and lack flexible control over compilation granularity.

    To overcome these challenges, we developed \texttt{qompiler}, a cross-platform quantum circuit synthesizer designed for traceability, verifiability, and configurable gate control.
    It decomposes Hamiltonians into circuits using a multi-stage framework including two-level, swap, control, Euler angle decompositions, and Solovay-Kitaev approximations.
    The intermediate representation (IL) is structured as a B-Tree, enabling gate-level lineage and is platform-independent, supporting major frameworks
    such as Qiskit, Cirq, and OpenQASM~\cite{cirq2024,qiskit2023,openqasm2,openqasm3}.
    Noticeably, the compiler allows fine-grained gate selection to match native hardware capabilities—for example,
    using Pauli gates on superconducting hardware or rotation gates on trapped-ion devices—facilitating hardware-aware optimization and broad applicability.


    \section{Method}\label{sec:method}
    The proposed compiler, \texttt{qompiler}, decomposes user-defined Hamiltonians into executable quantum circuits through a multi-stage decomposition framework.
    As a recap, given an input Hamiltonian $\hat{H}$, the quantum dynamics are fully prescribed by the time-dependent evolution operator
    \begin{equation}
        \label{eq:hamiltonian}
        \hat{U}\left( t \right) = e^{-i\hat{H}t/\hbar}
    \end{equation}
    In Schrödinger's representation, for example, the system state is given as
    \begin{equation}
        \label{eq:sysstate}
        \left\| \psi\left( t \right) \right> = \hat{U}\left( t \right)\left\| \psi_{0} \right>
    \end{equation}
    where $\left\| \psi_{0} \right>$ is the initial ket of an arbitrary system state.
    Whereas, most observable operators remain constant unless they are explicitly time-dependent.
    Equivalently, the formulation of the dynamics in Heisenberg's representation are
    \begin{equation}
        \label{eq:heisenberg}
        \hat{A} \left( t \right) = \hat{U}^{\dagger} \left( t \right) \hat{A}_{0} \hat{U} \left( t \right)
    \end{equation}
    where $\hat{A}_{0}$ is the initial operator for an arbitrary observable.
    Whereas, the state kets remain constants.

    For computational purposes, we express the evolution operator as a unitary matrix \( U \) on a suitably chosen basis,
    and this will be our starting point for this paper.
    We divide the overall workflow into two stages: compilation and interpretation.
    During compilation, \texttt{Qompiler} takes \( U \) as input and decomposes it into gate-level circuits expressed
    using an intermediate language (IL)
    designed by us.

    The compilation process comprises several key steps, including two-level decomposition (2L), swap gate decomposition (CX),
    control gate decomposition (CTRL), Euler rotation decomposition (EA), and the Solovay-Kitaev approximation
    (SK)~\cite{nielsen2010q,dawson2005sk}.
    All the decomposition processes are configurable, offering granularity control.

    Next are the optimization passes.
    These are the built-in processes that reduce and consolidate the redundant gates, overly fragmented circuits,
    and other non-optimal parts of the code.
    Think of the decomposition processes as `push` whereas these processes as `pull`.
    Through the optimization processes, we `pull back` some of the decomposition actions.
    By the actions in both the `push` and pull processes, we hope to find the optimal compilation result
    to suit the native constraints of targeted quantum hardware.

    The final compiled code (quantum circuit) is represented in a B-Tree data structure,
    which encodes gate-level lineage via parent-child relationships among tree nodes and thus robust traceability
    and verifiability which will be the topic of Section~\ref{sec:traceability-and-verifiability}.

    A key feature of \texttt{qompiler} is its configurable compilation granularity,
    which allows users to tailor the resulting circuit to the gate sets best suited for the target hardware.
    For instance, superconducting quantum hardware often implements Pauli gates ($X$, $Y$, $Z$) virtually,
    offering zero-error and zero-latency execution;
    in this case, the compiler can be configured to prioritize Pauli gates.
    Conversely, trapped-ion platforms such as IonQ natively support arbitrary single-qubit rotations,
    such as $R_x(\phi)$, $R_y(\phi)$, and $R_z(\phi)$, and the compiler can be adapted accordingly to produce rotation-based circuits.
    This hardware-aware configurability enables efficient, optimized circuit generation across diverse quantum backends
    while maintaining traceability and cross-platform compatibility.

    Finally, the interpretation stage.
    The compiler’s intermediate language (IL) is platform-independent and can be interpreted and rendered
    into executable circuits for specific quantum platforms.
    The IL can also be translated into other quantum software standards such as OpenQASM for broader
    compatibility~\cite{openqasm2,openqasm3}.
    This stage is designed to be highly adaptable and open to new platforms.
    We have demonstrated the interpretation with quantum computing platforms such as Qiskit and Cirq.
    Support for other platforms may be added easily with minimal programming.


    \section{Implementation}\label{sec:impl}
    In Figure~\ref{fig:impl}, we present the design of the compilation framework of \texttt{Qompiler}.
    The main flow is ``Compiler configuration → Operator decomposition → Optimization → Circuit rendering''.

        \caption{The design diagram of `\texttt{Qompiler}'.
        Rectangles (blue) represent major class constructs.
        Rectangles with a jagged bottom (purple) represent file input/output (I/O).
        Parallelograms (pink) represent data.
        The computer icon represents user inputs.
        }
        \label{fig:impl}
    \end{figure}

    The major compilation workflow in details in~\ref{fig:workflow}.
    The entry point is `Unitary`.
    If the configuration prescribed a granularity of \texttt{MULTI\_TARGET}, the decomposition will end with multi-target operators.
    Otherwise, the unitary operator will be decomposed, in turn, into 2-level unitary, singlet qubit, and control-pruned gates.
    Then a granularity probe of type \texttt{PRINCIPAL} is performed to determine if the target granularity has been reached.
    If yes, output Principal rotational operators.
    Otherwise, continue to break down the operators into either Clifford+T gate sets or customer-defined Universal gate sets.
    \begin{figure}[htbp]
        \centering
        \includegraphics[width=.8\linewidth]{workflow}
        \caption{The major compilation workflow in details.
        Each parallelogram (pink) represents a stage of the compiled data.
        Each rhombus (green) represents a granularity test.
        There are probes for three major granularity types---\texttt{MULTI\_TARGET}, \texttt{PRINCIPAL}, and \texttt{CLIFFORD\_T}.
        }
        \label{fig:workflow}
    \end{figure}

    The resulting IL is a B-Tree data structure and is written to \textit{.qco} files.
    In the B-Tree data structure, parent nodes generally represent the coarser grained operators
    and child nodes represent the decomposed operators,
    the products of which are just the parent nodes, e.g.,
    \begin{equation}
        \label{eq:parent-child-relation}
        \hat{P} = \prod_{i=1}^{n}{\hat{C}_{i}}.
    \end{equation}
    Generally speaking, the B-Tree data structure is layered and contains the following layers---2level unitary,
    single-qubit operators, control-pruned operators, and universal gates (including Clifford+T gates).

    \section{Traceability and Verifiability}\label{sec:traceability-and-verifiability}

    One of the key features of the current implementation of \texttt{qompiler} is its built-in feature---traceability and verifiability.
    When using quantum computing on real world applications, such as quantum chemistry, the size of quantum circuit can be easily huge.
    A quantum program for simulating a simple molecule like LiH in STO-3G solution would require $1000$ quantum gates~\cite{Hong_2022}.
    Simulation programs for moderately large molecules could consist of millions of quantum gates~\cite{poulin2014,google_science_2020}.
    It's daunting to keep track of all of these quantum gates and be sure of the correctness.
    \texttt{qompiler} is designed with traceability and verifiability in mind upfront.
    We organized the compiled code as a B-Tree data structure in the intermediate language.
    The traceability is built in the parent-child relationship.
    Each and every gate in the final program may be traced back to the specific part that produced it.
    The built-in optimizers used in the later stages of compilation also add tracing information regarding their operations.


    \section{Results}\label{sec:results}
    Initial benchmarks demonstrate that the compiler generates correct and consistent quantum circuits across multiple backends.
    These results highlight the compiler’s potential to accelerate quantum software development,
    circuit optimization, and experimentation across diverse quantum computing platforms.

    \section{Conclusion and Future Work}\label{sec:conclusion-and-future-work}
    Initial benchmarks confirm the correctness and consistency of the output across supported platforms, including Qiskit and Cirq.
    Future work includes incorporating parallelization to improve compilation efficiency for higher-dimensional Hamiltonians.
    We also plan to extend compatibility to additional frameworks, such as AWS Braket and PennyLane,
    with the broader goal of streamlining quantum algorithm development across the heterogeneous quantum computing
    landscape~\cite{braket-zenodo,pennylane}.
    In addition, we aim to integrate quantum error correction capabilities to enhance the reliability of synthesized circuits.
    We invite collaboration and feedback from the quantum software community.
