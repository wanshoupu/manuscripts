    \begin{abstract}
        We present a quantum software framework that bridges the gap between physics modeling and quantum software development.
        At the core of this framework is a versatile quantum compiler capable of decomposing arbitrary Hamiltonians into quantum circuits,
        represented using a platform-independent B-Tree-based intermediate language.
        The B-Tree structure encodes information for gate lineage, enabling detailed traceability of quantum circuit gates and facilitating code verification.
        The B-Tree-based intermediate language serves as a universal, hardware-agnostic carrier of compiled code,
        readily adaptable to multiple quantum hardware backends as well as other universal languages.
        We demonstrate rendering the compiled code into executable quantum circuits in Qiskit and Cirq.
        We can also transpile the compiled intermediate language into OpenQASM,
        a standard language for describing quantum circuits, for broader compatibility.
    \end{abstract}

    \section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

    Quantum simulation offers significant advantages in fields such as chemistry, pharmaceuticals, and biomedicine, materials science, etc.
    Given a Hamiltonian $\hat{H}$ as input, one can calculate the time-dependent evolution operator as
    \begin{equation}
        \label{eq:hamiltonian}
        \hat{U}\left( t \right) = e^{-i\hat{H}t/\hbar},
    \end{equation}
    where $t$ is time and $\hbar$ is Planck's constant.
    In Schrödinger picture, the state ket of the system is a function of time and given by
    \begin{equation}
        \label{eq:sysstate}
        \left\| \psi\left( t \right) \right\rangle = \hat{U}\left( t \right)\left\| \psi_{0} \right\rangle
    \end{equation}
    where $\left\| \psi_{0} \right\rangle$ is the initial ket of an arbitrary system state.
    Observables remain constant unless they are explicitly time-dependent.
    Equivalently in Heisenberg picture, the observables are time-dependent and formulated as
    \begin{equation}
        \label{eq:heisenberg}
        \hat{A} \left( t \right) = \hat{U}^{\dagger} \left( t \right) \hat{A}_{0} \hat{U} \left( t \right)
    \end{equation}
    where $\hat{A}_{0}$ is a constant operator for the observable at time $t=0$.
    The state kets remain constant.
    There are other presentation pictures than these two.
    But in all pictures, the quantum dynamics of the system are fully encoded in $\hat{U}\left( t \right)$.
    Simulation of a system is equivalent to simulate the operator $\hat{U}\left( t \right)$.
    For numerical calculation, we can choose a suitable basis for the Hilbert space and convert
    the evolution operator to a series of unitary matrix, $U_{t_i}$, for $i = 0, 1, 2, \dots$.


    Despite its potential in quantum simulation, developing quantum computing software is quite challenging and error-prone,
    requiring expertise in physics, mathematics, and software engineering.
    The steep learning curve poses a barrier and limits access for potential users and professionals
    who could benefit from quantum computing.
    Existing quantum transpilers are often platform-specific, implemented in an ad hoc manner,
    and offer limited control over compilation granularity.


    To address these challenges, we developed \texttt{Qompiler}, a cross-platform quantum circuit synthesizer
    featuring automation, mass-production, traceability, verifiability, and control.
    \texttt{Qompiler} decomposes the evolution operator, $U$, into quantum circuits
    using multi-stage decomposition processes.
    The final compiled code (quantum circuit) is represented as a B-Tree data structure,
    an intermediate language (QIL) which is the key for compilation lineage and platform independence.
    Through polymorphically implemented \texttt{CircuitBuilder}, we can realize cross-platform support including but not limited to
    Qiskit, Cirq, and OpenQASM~\cite{cirq2024,qiskit2023,openqasm2,openqasm3}.
    Notably, \texttt{Qompiler}'s powerful configuration capabilities enable fine-grained gate selection
    that aligns with the native hardware.
    For example, on superconducting hardware, standard Pauli gates may be more preferable than more complicated gates,
    whereas on trapped-ion devices, rotation gates can be efficiently implemented.
    This hardware-aware optimization is designed into the foundation of the cross-platform \texttt{Qompiler}.

    Let's go over the high-level design of \texttt{Qompiler}.
    The major functionalities of the system are organized into the following core modules:
    ``Configuration'', ``Decomposition'', ``Optimization'', and ``Rendering'' (See Figure~\ref{fig:impl}).
    We will briefly describe each of these modules here, while leaving detailed discussions to later sections.


    Rich controls of \texttt{Qompiler} are made possible through the sophisticated configuration system,
    offering control over almost all aspects of the compilation process,
    including but not limited to the granularity control, target platform, error tolerances,
    quantum device space allocation, optimization level, etc.
    This makes it possible for users to precisely tailor the gate granularity of the quantum circuit
    to best suit the targeted hardware.
    For instance, superconducting quantum hardware often implements Pauli gates ($X$, $Y$, $Z$) virtually,
    offering zero-error and zero-latency execution;
    So for superconducting quantum hardware, we can configure the compiler to prioritize Pauli gates.
    On the other hand, trapped-ion platforms natively support arbitrary single-qubit rotations,
    including $R_x(\phi)$, $R_y(\phi)$, and $R_z(\phi)$, and the compiler can be configured accordingly
    to produce rotation-based circuits.
    This hardware-aware configurability enables efficient, optimized circuit generation across diverse quantum backends
    while maintaining traceability and cross-platform compatibility.


    In the current design, \texttt{ConfigManager} is at the center of the configuration module.
    \texttt{ConfigManager} can read configuration inputs from several sources: default settings JSON file,
    custom overrides JSON file, and command line settings.
    Once all configuration sources are loaded and combined,
    the final settings are used to create a singleton instance of \texttt{QFactory},
    following a factory design pattern ~\cite{Gamma1994DesignPattern}.
    From the \texttt{QFactory}, other compilation constructs are created as
    needed---\texttt{Qompiler}, \texttt{QRenderer}, \texttt{QDevice}, \texttt{QConfig}, \texttt{Optimizers}, etc.


    Now that all the needed objects of \texttt{Qompiler} are in place, it can start to process inputs and
    compile gate-level quantum code.
    The compilation process comprises several key steps, including two-level decomposition (2L), swap gate decomposition (CX),
    control gate decomposition (CTRL), Euler rotation decomposition (EA),
    and the Solovay-Kitaev approximation (SK)~\cite{nielsen2010q,dawson2005sk}.
    The \texttt{Qompiler} class performs these steps recursively.
    We will spend the entire Section~\ref{sec:decomp} to discuss this as well as future development.


    The final compiled code (quantum circuit) is represented as a B-Tree data structure,
    an intermediate language (QIL) designed specifically for the task.
    QIL encodes gate-level lineage via parent-child relationships among tree nodes, thereby providing robust traceability
    and verifiability, which will be discussed in Section~\ref{sec:traceability-and-verifiability}.


    Nearing the end of the compilation process are the optimization passes
    where multiple passes may be taken to simplify the circuit output,
    including gate annihilation, reduction, and consolidation.
    Think of the decomposition processes as a `push' and the optimization processes as a `pull'.
    Through the optimization processes, we `pull back` some of the decomposition actions.
    By combining the actions in both the `push` and `pull` processes, we aim to find an optimal compilation result
    that respects the native constraints of the targeted quantum hardware.
    This will be the topic for Section~\ref{sec:optimization}.


    Once the compilation process is finished, the end result is binary code written to disk as `.qco' files.
    Finally, the interpretation stage involves the \texttt{QRenderer} and \texttt{CircuitBuilder},
    which handle circuit interpretation and rendering.
    The compiler’s intermediate language (QIL) is platform-independent and can be interpreted and rendered
    into executable circuits for specific quantum platforms.
    The QIL can also be translated into other quantum software standards, such as OpenQASM, for broader compatibility~\cite{openqasm2,openqasm3}.
    Note that the compilation is done once, but the circuit interpreting and rendering may be done any number of times,
    for any supported platforms.
    This stage is designed to be highly adaptable and open to new platforms.
    Currently supported quantum computing platforms include Qiskit and Cirq.
    Support for other platforms can be added easily by writing a circuit builder extending the base class \texttt{CircuitBuilder}.
    We will visit this in Section~\ref{sec:rendering}.


    \section{Design of Elementary Classes}\label{sec:design-of-elements}

    \begin{figure}[tbhp]
        \centering
        \includegraphics[width=.6\linewidth]{matrices-gates.pdf}

    In this section, we will lay the foundation of terminologies for discussions in this article.
    We will not go over all the elements for quantum simulation.
    Instead, we will focus on a few key elements of quantum circuit and refer readers to references such as~\cite{nielsen2010q}
    for the basics.
    Representations of unitary matrices and quantum gates, especially, controlled quantum gates,
    are the central part of the compiler design.
    A unitary matrix refers to a multidimensional complex matrix that is unitary.
    It captures the dynamics of a specific quantum system.
    It usually has no reference to any specific qubit for the simulation.
    A quantum gate, on the other hand, is a predefined unit of quantum operation acting on a specific domain of qubits.
    We call the specific domain of qubits a ``quantum space'' and refer to it as \texttt{qspace} in this article.
    The compilation process, which will be discussed in another section in more details,
    transfers the encoded dynamics of the unitary matrix to that of a network of quantum gates.

    \subsection{Unitary Matrices}\label{subsec:unitary-matrices}

    We created \texttt{UnitaryM} to represent unitary matrices.
    Given the sparse nature of the evolution operator, $\hat{U}\left( t \right)$,
    we designed \texttt{UnitaryM} readily accommodate sparsity and still be efficient.
    For example, for a $10$-qubit Hamiltonian system, it's rarely the case that all $10$ qubits interact with one another.
    More often, qubits next to one another interact.
    Reflected in the evolution operator, these correspond to the off-diagonal elements.


    Accordingly, we only store the rows and columns with non-identity diagonal elements and/or
    with non-zero off-diagonal elements.
    We call the indices of these rows and columns the ``core-indices''
    and the submatrix corresponding to these rows and columns the ``core-matrix''.
    We implemented the \texttt{matmul} method for matrix multiplication operations between \texttt{UnitaryM}'s.
    The class method \texttt{deflate} converts a NumPy array ($2$D) into \texttt{UnitaryM}.
    The method \texttt{inflate} (and \texttt{numpy.array}) does the opposite---returns
    the matrix in the format of \texttt{numpy} $2$D array.

    As stated earlier, a quantum gate differs from a unitary operator in that it is associated with a specific \texttt{qspace},
    whereas a unitary operator is not tied to any qubits.
    Each qubit in a \texttt{qspace} can assume one of three roles:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \texttt{TARGET}, whose quantum state may be altered.
        \item \texttt{CONTROL}, whose quantum state undergoes no change.
        It comes with two subtypes \texttt{CONTROL0} with activation value of zero ($0$) and \texttt{CONTROL1}
        with activation value of one ($1$).
        \item \texttt{IDLER}, who does not participate in the gate operation at all.
    \end{itemize}
    For clarity, we introduce the notion of `granularity' as a classification of quantum gates,
    which can be roughly characterized by the following factors:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item The number of qubits involved
        \item The roles assigned to each qubit, e.g., \texttt{TARGET} vs.\ \texttt{CONTROL}.
        \item Core operator features, including standard vs.\ nonstandard types and the types of formulation, e.g., rotation around principal axis
    \end{itemize}

    We listed all the major types of quantum gates in Table~\ref{tab:terminology} for ease of reference.
    For all the types of quantum gates, we designed one class `\texttt{CtrlGate}'---single qubit or
    multi-qubit, single-target or multi-target, with or without control qubits.

    The \texttt{CtrlGate} can be built from Pauli operators, $X$, $Y$, $Z$, Hadamard operator $H$, phase operators, $S$, $T$,
    and their Hermite conjugates, rotational matrices, $R_x(\phi)$, $R_y(\phi)$, and $R_z(\phi)$, or any arbitrary unitary matrices.
    The \texttt{CtrlGate} class is also the workhorse of most operations involving controlled gates:
    matrix multiplications, `\texttt{matmul}', expanding into extra Hilbert space, qubit sorting,
    trace operation, and direct conversion of \texttt{UnitaryM} into \texttt{CtrlGate}.


    As keen readers have noticed, \texttt{IDLER} qubits are degrees of freedom that can be added into
    or taken away from the formulation of \texttt{CtrlGate}.
    Expanding into additional Hilbert space is crucial when multiplying multi-qubit operators that span different qubit spaces.
    Adding extra qubits to \texttt{CtrlGate} is implemented by the method \texttt{expand}.
    By performing successive Kronecker product operations, one can inject idler qubits in between the original qubit spaces.


    Another operation, useful especially for circuit verification, is the \texttt{trace} operation.
    Because of the use of ancilla qubits, we need to eliminate these qubits from the multi-qubit gates
    in order to compare with the input gate.
    Without the trace operation, verification of the compilation process would be challenging.
    Class diagrams for the two classes are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:um-and-cgate}.


    \section{Operator Decomposition}\label{sec:decomp}

    The compilation process transfers the encoding of the dynamics from the input $n \times n$ unitary
    matrix into a network of quantum gates, allocates and assigns qubits to each gate.
    In this section, we go over the decomposition process of the \texttt{Qompiler}.


    Starting from an $n$-dimensional unitary operator, the quantum formulation undergoes several decomposition processes
    until the resulting quantum gates reach the desired granularity, such as principal gates or \texttt{CNOT}.
%    The quantum circuit attains the original quantum dynamics with $100\%$ fidelity.
    The main decomposition processes used by \texttt{Qompiler} are listed here:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \texttt{mat2l\_decompose}: decomposes an arbitrary $n \times n$ unitary matrix into \texttt{2level} unitary matrices.
        \item \texttt{cnot\_decompose}: decomposes a \texttt{2level} unitary matrix into single-target operators in universal gates.
        \item \texttt{ctrl\_decompose}: given a controlled gate, break its control sequence down to shorter controls,
        usually with the help of ancilla qubits.
        \item \texttt{euler\_decompose}: decomposes a single-qubit gate into Euler rotation operators and optionally a phase shift.
        \item \texttt{cliffordt\_decompose}: given a universal gate, decomposes it into \texttt{Clifford+T} gates.
        \item \texttt{sk\_decompose}: Based on Solovay-Kitaev Theorem, approximates a single-qubit gate into \texttt{universal} gates
        or a subset of it, e.g., the \texttt{Clifford+T} gates.
    \end{itemize}

        \centering
        \includegraphics[width=.8\linewidth]{workflow.pdf}

    The class \texttt{Qompiler} orchestrates all decomposition processes recursively in a depth-first manner.
    The granularity of the final quantum gate is specified in the \texttt{QConfig}.
    As illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:workflow},
    unless the configuration specifies a granularity of \texttt{MULTI\_TARGET},
    the \texttt{Qompiler} will perform \texttt{mat2l\_decompose} and \texttt{cnot\_decompose},
    to decompose the gates into \texttt{2level} unitary operators and \texttt{Singlet} gates, correspondingly.
    Next, the \texttt{Qompiler} will determine if the granularity specification requires pruning the control sequence.
    If so, it will perform \texttt{ctrl\_decompose} followed by \texttt{euler\_decompose}
    to break down into \texttt{PRINCIPAL} gates.
    If \texttt{PRINCIPAL} gate types are acceptable, the decomposition process will end.
    Otherwise, the \texttt{sk\_decompose} may be performed to approximate the single-qubit gate
    with a sequence of universal gates.
    (The \texttt{sk\_decompose} will be discussed in Section~\ref{sec:sk-approx} in more details.)
    Then the \texttt{Qompiler} will continue to test if \texttt{Clifford+T} gate is required, and
    if so, the \texttt{cliffordt\_decompose} is performed to conform to the required gate types.


    By the end of decomposition processes, all the gates are guaranteed
    to meet or exceed the configured granularity level.
    It is the job of optimizers to tune back the over-decomposed gates,
    and that's the subject of Section~\ref{sec:optimization}.


    \section{Solovay--Kitaev Approximation}\label{sec:sk-approx}

    Solovay and Kitaev Theorem is a piece of foundational work in the quantum computation field~\cite{kitaev1997quantum,dawson2005sk}.
    Thanks to this theorem, it is possible to efficiently approximate any single-qubit operator
    from the \texttt{SU(2)} group using a set of universal gates.
    With a predefined error margin $\epsilon$, both the number of gates to approximate the operator
    and the runtime to calculate them as a function of $\epsilon$ are
    polylogarithmic, $~O(\log^{c}(1/\epsilon))$ with $c$ a constant.


    The basic idea behind Solovay \& Kitaev approximation from the above references is understandably simple.
    Over the operator space in the \texttt{SU(2)} group, create a point net such that the distance between adjacent points is bound by $\epsilon_{0}$,
    an error margin of a certain threshold (referred to as \texttt{SU2Net}).
    At zeroth order, operators near a point in the \texttt{SU2Net} are approximated using the gate sequence associated with that point.
    Then call the function \mintinline{python}{def sk_decompose(self, U: NDArray, depth: int) -> Bytecode:}
    with a recursion depth according to the desired error margin $\epsilon$.
    \texttt{sk\_decompose} is a recursive function whose base case is simply the zero-th order approximation provided by the \texttt{SU2Net}.
    For each recursion, the group commutator of the residual operator is calculated and used for better approximation
    until it is close enough to the identity operator $I$.

    The caveat of the Solovay--Kitaev decomposition lies in selecting a properly constructed \texttt{SU2Net}.
    For experimentation, we built several variants of SU2 nets, each based on different operator distance metrics,
    including \texttt{kD-tree}, \texttt{nearest-neighbor} with ball-tree, and a custom Kolmogorov distance.
    Despite our attempts to balance accuracy and runtime, our SK decomposition
    implementation is not yet efficient enough for production use.
    Improving this remains a potential direction for future work.

            \caption{Same circuit diagram with basic optimization passes.
            Some gates are completely annihilated in the pass.
            The consolidated gates are framed.}
            \label{fig:optimized-circuit}
        \end{subfigure}
        \label{fig:optimize-comparison}
    \end{figure}


    Earlier, we briefly mentioned the use of optimizers to ``pull back'' some of the over-decomposed quantum gates.
    We elaborate on this now.


    In the decomposition processes, the \texttt{Qompiler} aims to ensure the resulting gates are $\geq$ the designated granularity.
    This often produces consecutive gates that annihilate one another or gates with smaller-than-desired granularity.
    To compensate for this, we included optimization passes that act as a synthesis process to simplify the output circuit,
    combining multiple operators into fewer, more efficient gates.
    Shown in Figure~\ref{fig:optimize-comparison} is a side-by-side comparison of the same circuit diagram
    with and without the optimization passes.
    This push-and-pull design not only maximizes the efficiency of the resulting circuit
    but also provides precise control over gate granularity.


    \begin{figure}[tbhp]
        \centering
        \centerline{\includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{optimizer-render}}
        \caption{Renderer and Optimizer class diagrams.}
        \label{fig:optimizer-renderer-class-design}
    \end{figure}


    Two optimizer classes are used to optimize the quantum circuit used in the figure, \texttt{AnnihilationOptimizer}
    and \texttt{ConsolidationOptimizer}, both of the \texttt{SlidingWindowOptimizer} family
    which detects optimization opportunities among the gates within a window of certain size
    and performs such optimizations.
    The \texttt{SlidingWindowOptimizer} is derived from the \texttt{Optimizer} interface
    which declares the optimization level and the optimize method.
    The class relationships for the optimizers are provided in UML diagram~\ref{fig:optimizer-renderer-class-design}.


    Furthermore, our compiler design can easily accommodate custom optimizers.
    For example, if a particular gate type, like arbitrary single-qubit rotations, is more efficient on certain platforms,
    like IonQ Forte\textregistered,
    an optimizer can be added to the compiler to consolidate gates into rotational gates to exploit this advantage.
    After each of the optimizers takes a pass on the compiled data structure, \texttt{ByteCode},
    the resulting \texttt{ByteCode} is written to a binary-format output file \texttt{.qco}.


    \section{Traceability and Verifiability}\label{sec:traceability-and-verifiability}

    When applying quantum computing to real-world problems, such as quantum chemistry,
    the size of the quantum circuit can become very large.
    A quantum program for simulating a simple molecule like LiH in the STO-3G basis
    may require approximately $1000$ gates~\cite{Hong_2022}.
    Simulation programs for moderately large molecules can consist of millions~\cite{poulin2014,google_science_2020}.
    Keeping track of all these gates and ensuring correctness is challenging.


    \begin{figure}[tbhp]
        \centering
        \begin{overpic}[width=0.6\textwidth]{btree.pdf}
            \put(0,70){\colorbox{white}{\makebox(20,15){}}}
        \end{overpic}
        \caption{The layered structure of, \texttt{ByteCode}, B-Tree data structure.
        The root of the tree is the input unitary matrix,
            followed by intermediate layers: two-level unitary, single-qubit, control-pruned.
            At the leave layer, are the components of the quantum circuit.
        }
        \label{fig:btree}
    \end{figure}


    We highlighted the built-in support for traceability and verifiability of our quantum compiler system
    in Section~\ref{sec:introduction}.
    Here, we examine how this is achieved.
    To store and organize compiled code in the intermediate language, we designed a data structure called \texttt{ByteCode}.
    \texttt{ByteCode} is a B-Tree, where each node represents a stage of the compiled quantum operator
    along with associated metadata.
    Traceability is encoded in the parent--child relationships:
    each gate in the final program can be traced back to the specific operation that produced it.
    The built-in optimizers applied in later stages of compilation also record tracing information for their operations,
    preserving the original lineage.


    In the B-Tree data structure, parent nodes generally correspond to coarser-grained operators,
    whereas child nodes are finer-grained operators whose matrix product recovers the parent node, e.g.,
    \begin{equation}
        \label{eq:parent-child-relation}
        \hat{P} = \prod_{i=1}^{n} \hat{C}_{i}.
    \end{equation}
    An example of a B-Tree is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:btree} for illustration of the layered nature.


    Convenient methods such as \texttt{is\_leaf} and \texttt{herm} (Hermitian conjugate) are provided
    to simplify programming with tree nodes.
    Two iterators, \texttt{PreorderIterator} and \texttt{ReversePreorderIterator}, are also implemented
    to traverse the tree.
    In addition, we support serialization/deserialization (\texttt{serde}) of this data structure,
    to faithfully store and load the B-Tree data structure to and from \textit{.qco} files.

    With the tracking and tracing information of the \texttt{ByteCode} data structure by design, verification of the quantum circuit
    and program debugging is made easier in \texttt{Qompiler}.


    \section{Circuit Rendering}\label{sec:rendering}

    The end product of the compilation process is the code file `\texttt{.qco}'.
    Rendering the code file into a quantum circuit on the target platform is the responsibility of
    \texttt{Renderer} and related circuit building classes.
    The guiding paradigm of the \texttt{Qompiler} is: ``Compile once, render multiple times, and use everywhere.''
    Accordingly, the \texttt{Renderer} class is designed to accommodate polymorphic implementations
    of the \texttt{CircuitBuilder} interface.
    We provide two implementations of \texttt{CircuitBuilder}, namely, \texttt{QiskitBuilder} for
    Qiskit\textregistered compatible platforms and \texttt{CirqBuilder} for Google Cirq\textregistered platforms.
    The UML diagram for the rendering classes is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:optimizer-renderer-class-design}.
    More support for other platforms can be easily added simply by new implementations of the \texttt{CircuitBuilder} interface.


    Qubit allocation and mapping are central topics in quantum computing.
    We distinguish between two types of \texttt{qspace} allocation.
    The first is the allocation of virtual \texttt{qspace}, and the second is the allocation of physical \texttt{qspace}.
    The mapping between them is carried out during circuit rendering.
%    When we compile the unitary operator into QIL, we allocated virtual qubits.
    Allocation of virtual \texttt{qspace} is performed at compilation time and managed by the \texttt{QDevice}.
    Allocation of physical qubits, together with the mapping of virtual qubits onto them, on the other hand,
    is tied to a specific target platform and can only take place at circuit render time.
    Qubit allocation is a crucial step and can be highly sophisticated, as it underpins the feasibility of computation
    and strongly affects performance.
    An effective physical qubit allocation algorithm should take qubit interactions into account.
    In this article, however, we are not focusing on this aspect.
    Instead, we adopt a simplified approach to illustrate the concept.
    The study of advanced qubit allocation algorithms is left for future work.


    \section{Testing}\label{sec:tests}

    Ensuring correctness is fundamental to the success of \texttt{Qompiler} project.
    We want the compiled circuit to be $100\%$ correct and approximations to fall within the predetermined error margin.
    To achieve this goal, we vigorously test the project against all encountered edge cases.
    Most major constructs in \texttt{Qompiler} have nearly $100\%$ test coverage.
    The overall unit test coverage of the whole project also reaches $90\%$.


    To facilitate testing, we designed a couple of utility classes and functions such as the \texttt{MatrixFormatter}
    and the numerous functions in the module \texttt{mgen}, etc.
    It is worth mentioning the \texttt{inter\_product} function,

    Despite these successes, the \texttt{Qompiler} project has certain limitations.
    For example, the project was implemented in Python with numerical libraries, including NumPy and SciPy.
    Therefore, the underlying numerical errors limit the precision of the compiled quantum circuit.
    However, when the Solovay--Kitaev approximation is employed,

    The program can become computationally intensive for large unitary matrices
    The memory footprint may also become a bottleneck once the number of qubits reaches a certain threshold.
    It is expected that the pressure on computational resources can be alleviated through distributed computation.


    \section{Conclusion and Future Work}\label{sec:conclusion-and-future-work}
    We presented a configurable, cross-platform quantum compiler, \texttt{Qompiler}, featuring built-in gate traceability.
    Initial benchmarks confirm the correctness and consistency of \texttt{Qompiler}'s
    output across supported platforms, including Qiskit and Cirq.
    Future work will focus on distributed computation and parallelization to enable the
    compilation of large input matrices and to improve overall efficiency.
    We plan to extend compatibility to additional frameworks, such as AWS Braket and PennyLane,
    with the broader goal of streamlining quantum algorithm development across the heterogeneous quantum
    computing landscape~\cite{braket-zenodo,pennylane}.
    We are also considering implementing sophisticated qubit allocation algorithms
    to adapt to given topologies and interaction strategies such as $1$D linear chain,
    $2$D grid, star, all-to-all, etc.
    Furthermore, we aim to incorporate quantum error correction techniques to enhance the reliability of synthesized circuits.
    We welcome collaboration and feedback from the quantum software community.
