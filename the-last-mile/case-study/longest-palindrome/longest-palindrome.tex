\chapter{Longest palindrome}

\section{The problem statement}

Given a string \code{S}, find the longest palindromic substring in \code{S}.

\section{Analysis of the algorithm}\label{sec:Manacher.algorithm}

Although algorithm is not the focus, you need a good understanding of it before moving on to the
rest of the chapter.
The optimal algorithm for detecting the longest palindromic substring in a string originates from
idea presented in a paper by Glenn Manacher~\cite{Manacher:1975}.
The basic idea originates from reflection symmetry in one-dimensional ($1$D) space,
\textit{a.k.a.}, mirror-image symmetry
\footnote{\url{https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reflection_symmetry}}.
Reflection symmetry in $1$D space refers to space invariance under reflection transformation.
A reflection transformation is the operation that transforms coordinates to their
mirror-image with respect to the fixed point, which we will refer as axis of symmetry or center
of symmetry.
Particularly, for a coordinate at $x$, its transformed coordinate $x^\prime$ \textit{w.r.t.} axis $a$ is:
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:reflection-transform}
    x^\prime = 2a - x.
\end{equation}

As shown in \autoref{fig:a-axis-reflection-symmetry}, axis of symmetry $A$ partitions the entire $1$D
space into left half-space and right half-space about itself, and points in the half-spaces have
one-to-one mapping.
Axis $B$ does similarly except \textit{w.r.t.} its own axis of symmetry
(\autoref{fig:b-axis-reflection-symmetry}).
If the two axes of reflection symmetry are simultaneously present along the $x$-axis, by
repetitively applying the reflection transformation, one may find infinitely many axes
periodically located along the $x$-axis and they partition the entire space into periodic regions
with period $2d$, where $d$ is the distance between the two axes of symmetry (see
\autoref{fig:two-axes-reflection-symmetry}).
You may have experienced such an illusion by stepping into two parallel mirrors \ldots

The palindromic property of string is a manifestation of reflection symmetry as shown in
\autoref{fig:palindrome-symmetry}.
In reality space may be limited so are strings.
Nevertheless as long as the regions of two reflection symmetry overlaps,
the above periodic argument still applies within the overlapping regions.

Now let us go back to the palindromic substring which may be viewed as discrete $1$D space.
Note that the center of symmetry of a palindrome can be either a \code{char} or
between two \code{char}s in the string.
To facilitate coding and discussion, we treat the left end and right end of the string as two
special centers and the length of the palindromic substrings at these two centers are always zero.
Furthermore, the left and right ends collapse into one in case of empty string.
So all in all, there are $(2 * n + 1)$ possible centers, where $n$ is length of the string.
On any center, the longest palindrome can be found by extending the left and right
boundaries as far as the equally distant pairs of \code{char}s match.
With all the palindromes, nearby palindromes may overlap with one another.
Some smaller palindromes may be nested within bigger ones.
Interesting thing would happen if a \code{char} belongs to several palindromes at
once because corresponding reflection transformations may be applied in any combination, any
order, and thus produce a group of points consisting of the original \code(char), hops, double-hops,
triple-hops, \textit{etc.}
Multiple reflection symmetries applied together on one \code{char} would produce the same effect as
you step into a pair of parallel mirrors---a multitude of you are present at
reflectively symmetric spots along the line.

This discussion of the multiple reflection symmetry is the key to understand Manacher's algorithm.

Take the string ``bananas'' as an example.
If one writes down the lengths of palindromes as an array, one would get something like this:
[0, 1, 0, 1, 0, 3, 0, 5, 0, 3, 0, 1, 0, 1, 0]
Do you notice the symmetry?
A long palindrome ``anana'' tucks under its left and right wings shorter palindromes
``ana''---exactly as we discussed above.
So when we check for palindrome substrings, we can skip the portion of substrings in one of
the wings.

It's common practice that one iterate through the string from left to right and as one does so,
one caches the result, \textit{e.g.} the length.
When examining a new center, the cached information for certain mirror image of it may be useful to
totally or partially determine the palindrome length.

In one loop, there are two frontiers: center and rightmost checked char.
within the right wing as we scan down the centers.

In some implementations, the original array is augmented by inserting
padding chars in between each pair of adjacent chars in the original string and require the
palindrome substrings be centered on one of the \code{char} of the augmented string (padding
\code{char} or original \code{char} alike).
So for \code{string} ``bananas'', the augmented array would be

    \begin{center}
        \caption{\label{tbl:breakdown-of-conditions}}
        \begin{tabular}{|c|c|c|c|c|c|c|c|c|c|c|c|c|}

Then all the palindromic substrings are centered on one of the \code{char} in the augmented
string for sake of implementation.
Algorithm outline is as follows, where $PS(i)$ denotes the longest palindrome
substring centered at \code{i}, and $LPS(i)$ denotes length of it.
\begin{clist}
    \caption{Algorithm for calculating the length of longest palindrome substrings based on
    Manacher's algorithm}
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item Initialize an array \code{lengths} of size $2 * n + 1$ that stores $LPL(i)$,
        with \code{i} being one of the indexes of the array;
        \item Initialize \code{maxCenter = 0}, which stores the palindrome center whose right wing
        reaches right-most in each iteration of the main loop;
        \item For each \code{char} at \code{j = 0, 1, ..., n} in the augmented array,
        \begin{enumerate}[label={\roman*:}]
            \item If $PS(j)$ lies within the right wing of $PS(\code{maxCenter})$,
            check the $PS(j^\prime)$, where $j^\prime$ is the mirrored center
            \textit{w.r.t.} \code{maxCenter}.
            If $PS(j^\prime)$ is completely tucked under the $PS(\code{maxCenter})$'s left wing,
            then the $LPS(j)$ is same as $PS(j^\prime)$.
            \item Otherwise, if part of $PS(j^\prime)$ protrudes from
            the left wing of $PS(\code{maxCenter})$,
            then we can inherit the part under the left wing and all
            we need is to calculate the portion protruding from the right wing of $PS
            (\code{maxCenter})$.
            \item Otherwise, we have to calculate from scratch the length of the longest
            palindrome centered on \code{j}.
        \end{enumerate}
    \end{enumerate}
\end{clist}

\section{Implementation}

In the literature, there are several implementations already
published, for example the one of CS department of Princeton
University\footnote{\url{https://algs4.cs.princeton.edu/53substring/Manacher.java.html}}.

There the prescription of the algorithm was literally translated into code---the original string
ws preprocessed to fill with blanks.

But with a little maneuver, one can create a `virtual filling' among the characters of the
original string and save on physical memory footprint.
