Years ago, when my research work came to a complete stall, I was convinced that I had no future
as an unachieved physicist.
Digging dreams of my youth time, I was delighted to find that I may be able to make a living if I
put up a different mindset and switch track from physics research to computer programming.
Through a prolonged, self-guided learning, I incrementally acquired higher and higher proficiency
in software engineering skills.
From a mere dilettante to an experienced professional it took me one decade.
Today my understanding in various aspects of software development is on a
commensurable standing with that of physics.
Furthermore, I have aggregated considerable comparative understandings between science and
engineering.

\section{Who am I?}\label{sec:whoAmI}

I'm a physics dropout who broke into software engineering field a decade ago.
%Unlike other physics dropouts, I decently spent enough time to finish my PhD thesis.
I didn't make ground-breaking discoveries in my research.
I have no dazzling publications on Science, Nature, or Cells.
Miraculously, I managed to close a page and flipped to the next.

So that little research background doesn't lend me much credentials for what I~am doing here.
However sharing my credentials is not my focus here here.
Share my struggles is.
As it came to pass, I am a self-taught software engineer and
I struggled for the past ten years to get to the bottom of everything that bothers me, ever since
I began to seriously consider computer programming as a career, the very first moment of my
attempt to understand the basics of algorithms, data structures, \textit{etc.}.
I spent hours and hours of focused time on comprehend the mechanisms, relationships, and analogies.
I built new learning on top of what I already mastered.
For instance, I managed to gain a personal, multi-dimensional understanding of why
pairwise-comparison-based sorting algorithms are bound by $Nlog(N)$ time complexity through long
time back-and-forth reflection.
While on it, I pondered on the similarity between the time complexity of sorting algorithms and
entropy of an $N$-particle isolated system.
I tracked it to Shannon's information entropy\cite{Shannon-mathematical-1948}, and from there
further up, all the way back to Boltzmann's development in statistical mechanics\cite{landau:1980}.

My immersion in this field was materialized in thousands of pages of notes, $40k$
lines of coding problems practices that I can find online\footnote{\textit{e.g.},
\url{https://projecteuler.net}, \url{https://leetcode.com}} or offline, and $100k+$ lines of
deployed production code at top-tier tech firms.
Many of the problems that I worked on were revisited for four to five times over a period of
several years and each time I was able to gain new understandings regarding why my code worked or
not worked and how to redo it in a cleaner way than before.
I believe the stumbles, the struggles, the lessons, and the learning is worth sharing by a book.
My learned experience shared here may not not eliminate bugs from your code.
But they will help eliminate hiding places for potential bugs.

\section{Why this book?}\label{sec:whyThisBook}

I didn't take this seriously until fairly recently when
I came to realize high-end software developers
have remained rare a commodity through so many years of tech hypes.
This anecdotal observation seems to be silently confirmed by repositories at
Github\footnote{\url{github.com}}.
Take a look at the source code of projects which are the bread and butter and are
depended by millions such as Hadoop, Spark, Apache Common Libraries, \textit{etc}.
Except the getters, setters and other boilerplate code, in any fairly dense implementation of
class, a method, it is almost guaranteed to contain one or more of the numerous `smells' as listed
in Martin Fowler's seminal book `Refactor'~\cite{Fowler1999}.

and convince yourself that many of these great project came with a great design but were
implemented with mediocrity.

You will see that they came with great designs but were only implemented with mediocrity.

Personally, I've worked with graduates and post-graduates from top-tier schools.
They come with latest theories in computer sciences.
Their resumes are full of buzz words and eye-catching GPA\@.
But at the end of the day when it's time to code up ideas, the incompetence betrays them
glaringly.

It is revealed in design reviews, code reviews, technical interviews in top-tier tech companies.

These plain facts convey the crying signal that there is a lack of supply of quality engineers.
As news has it, since software industry became an industry,
the gap between supply and demand for qualified professionals has been steadily growing.
This shortage nowadays takes a new form, \textit{e.g.}, a shortage of quality.

This means it is not a mere lack of ``engineers'', but the rarity of experienced engineers with a
deep understanding of software engineering.
This observation is simultaneously corroborated by authoritative research firms.
According to a 2018 research by Forrester\footnote{\url{https://go.forrester
.com/research/predictions}},
it cites that the prolonged software engineering talent shortage has turned from lack-of-quantity
to lack-of-quality and employers may end up paying $20\%$ above market for quality engineering
talent.

Therefore, this is what happens in reality: a few top-notch minds sense a need and conceive a
project to meet the need.
They then come up with a blueprint, a high-level design.
Then over a period of time, they managed to find talents of varying
proficiency levels and give it a faulty, smelly implementation followed by years of bug
fixing and maintenance.
It's a pity to see a world-class design having to put up with a mediocre implementation followed
by a painful, lengthy bug fixing trail.

I don't believe that implementation doesn't need top-notch software engineers to complete.
It is exactly the shortage of quality talents that causes this do-or-die makeshift.

Formal computer science education is a foundation but not the commodity of trade.
The application of education to problem solving is a skill that is learned by experience and
mentorship.
It is the main focus of this book to spread the insights I gained on my way toward sharpening my
software development and problem-solving skills.

What I want to achieve through this book is that through repeated retrospection and
self-criticism, one immerse in a learning mindset to achieve a progressively higher
level of proficiency.

\section{Who should read this book?} \label{sec:whoshouldread}

To the readers, many chapters in this book may seem familiar at first sight.
However it is the author's hope that the moment you start dive in the chapter, you may
immediately find something novel and interesting.

This book is intended for professionals who already have a solid command on
algorithms, data structures, operating systems, and programming languages of their choice.
However applying these to a complex coding practice on a daily basis remains a challenging task.
toward their software engineering career goal.

This book is rather a summary of 10 years software engineering experience of the author.
The author believes and have proved through personal experience the effectiveness of constant
learning.
Undeniably, a lot of activities help one improve on programming skills.
Coding up a great deal of problems will get you familiar with various algorithms, a command of
data structures, and brush up on your coding skills.
However, an often ignored activity is to contemplate on algorithms and your implementations of them.
From time to time, one need to look back on the works they did before and pause on
anything that may catch one's eyes.
Trying new things that ranges from mundane variable renaming to systematic code overhaul.

But the beauty of this kind of activities lies in the gifts that come with practice.
The more you explore, the more there is to discover.
If you make this a habit, you will unearth many gems in the depth of your ability.
You will be a perpetual generator of innovations, a powerhouse of self-sustained improvement.

Below is just a list of things that I usually spend time for meditation:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Bugs made previously
    \item Bugs fixed previously
    \item Why a particular implementation works
    \item Why a particular implementation does not work
    \item Refactor redundant code
    \item
\end{itemize}

\section{What this book is not about}\label{sec:thisBookIsNotAbout}

Some disclaimers are due here.

Firstly this book is \emph{not} a systematic programming primer.
Rather than focusing on complete coverage of most programming aspects,
it comprises of a scarce of organic programming experiences, home-made software
development techniques, and other observed patterns that are based on years of personal immersion
in the software industry and many of them are not talked about elsewhere.

Also this book is not squarely focused on interview problem-solving skills.
Problem-solving in a interview settings often emphasizes too much on `getting the job done'.
In a limited time, one is expected to understand and analyze the requirement, brainstorming for
possible solutions, design, algorithm picking and tailoring, data structure fitting, \textit{etc.}
Getting the job done in such a setting is at most biased if used solely to identify the right
candidate.

It inevitably fails to evaluate candidate for the skill set truly needed at work
such as code refactor and reuse.

Instead, interview setting should be as close to real work environment as possible.

Through an interview, we want to be sure that candidates can or have the potential to produce
usable, reusable, and readable code for large scale production.

The focus of this writing is to help beginning and intermediate developers to advance their
coding skills to next level.
The techniques discussed here are practical in production coding environment.
But they are not necessarily helpful during interview settings.
Therefore this book may be an overkill for interview prep.

I am not proposing new rules or new principles.
Many of the principles, programming patterns already exists.
What I attempt to do here is to show you how to turn these principles to correct and elegant code.
My ways are not to be accepted but to be felt and verified in your own practice.

Other than that, this book does not teach you any specific programming language.
It assumes readers have familiarity with the popular programming languages such as \python, \java,
C++ \textit{etc.}.
Neither does this book focus on algorithms or data structures, at least not systematically.
Where needed, the workings of an algorithms are outlined for reader's convenience.
That should be taken as the focus of the book.

This book is not about recipe or cookbook for any specific framework or system.
Rather, it's a general guideline for development with all frameworks and systems.

Last but not least, this book is not just about technical interviews.
Those who are preparing for technical interviews will benefit by reading this book.
But the main target of this book are those who are determined for long-term investment
of improving their programming skills.
Nevertheless, here and there, I use interview problems to illustrate the strategy of
implementation of certain algorithms.
But neither coding questions nor their solutions are the focus of this book.
The strategy of implementing functions and classes is not only applicable to coding questions
for an interview, but more so for software development at work.

Last but not least, it takes years of combat training in real battle fields to achieve a higher
level of proficiency.
It is, therefore, not my pursuance to help you cut short the path.

Finally, I hope the reader would take this book as a seed rather than food, as an inspiration for
endeavors rather than sharing of achievement for applause.
