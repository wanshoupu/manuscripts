Before moving on the main business of this part,
I'd like to draw an analogous comparison between sciences and engineering
which I believe is both amusing and useful for motivation.
In his book, ``The Sciences of the Artificial'', Nobel laureate Herbert A.\
Simon characterized a comparison between science and engineering as `` The natural sciences are concerned
with how things are \ldots Design, on the other hand,
is concerned with how things ought to be''~\cite{Herbert-Simon-1996}.
As two major domains of studies, science and engineering are built on common ground-mathematics.
Science is a crude, intricate cavern-stony, uncushioned, undecorated inside and out.
Engineering, on the other hand, is a mansion, developed modern dwelling place, for better or
worse.

The cavern of science permits only law-abiding visitors-no alteration is allowed or possible-and
visitors are bound to the open space amid rocks.
The mansion of engineering is permissive, open to changes.
If so desired, windows can be broken in, holes can be bored, walls can be torn, albeit at a price.
% with a consequence
The sole mission of scientists is to map out the limits, no human artifacts.
The mansion of engineering, however, is relenting.
Engineers enjoy the freedom of designing and creating artifacts-they can repurpose, expand,
remodel, or even rebuild the mansion, in part or in whole, as needed.
There is no stark boundary between forbidden and allowed.
There is no sheer right or wrong.
Everything is negotiable.
Everything has a price tag.

But not every changes are created equal.
Some changes are implausible by natural laws, some changes are non-viable due to practical reasons,
some changes cause severe problems years later, \textit{etc}.
The consequences are unknown at time of design, take long to manifest, and when they do, cause
long-lasting effect.
Engineering is, in a way, like parking a locomotive with no brake.

The vast amount of engineering possibilities, \textit{e.g.}, boring holes, opening windows,
building walls, leave a vast optimization space.


As a branch of engineering, software development is of no exception.
The solution space of software design is humongous.
The evaluation of design and implementation of software is probabilistic and time consuming.
The lack of stark, timely feedback in software engineering leaves best practices and mediocre
practices blended.

This motivates me to share those hard-learned lessons I had acquired over the years to be tested
by the reader.
