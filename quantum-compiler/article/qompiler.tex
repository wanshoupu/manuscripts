    \begin{abstract}
        We present a quantum system software framework that bridges the gap between physics modeling and quantum software development.
        At the core of this framework is a versatile quantum compiler capable of decomposing arbitrary Hamiltonians into quantum circuits, represented using a platform-independent B-Tree intermediate language (IL).
        The B-Tree structure provides essential lineage information for circuit gate traceability and code verification.
        The IL offers a universal, hardware-agnostic representation that enables flexible translation and optimization across diverse quantum hardware backends.
        We demonstrate implementations that render the IL into executable quantum circuits targeting platforms such as Qiskit and Cirq.
        Additionally, we provide a transpiler that converts the IL into OpenQASM for broader compatibility.
    \end{abstract}

    \section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

    Quantum simulation offers significant advantages for applications in chemistry, pharmaceuticals, and biological processes.
    Despite this potential, developing quantum software remains highly challenging, requiring expertise in physics, mathematics, and software engineering.
    This steep learning curve limits access for many users and professionals who could benefit from quantum computing.
    Furthermore, existing quantum transpilers are often platform-specific, implemented in an ad hoc manner, and provide limited control over compilation granularity.

    To address these challenges, we developed \texttt{qompiler}, a cross-platform quantum circuit synthesizer designed for traceability, verifiability, and configurable gate control.
    It decomposes Hamiltonians into circuits using a multi-stage framework, including two-level, swap, control, Euler angle decompositions, and Solovay-Kitaev approximations.
    The intermediate representation (IL) is structured as a B-Tree, enabling gate-level lineage and platform independence, and it supports major frameworks such as Qiskit, Cirq, and OpenQASM~\cite{cirq2024,qiskit2023,openqasm2,openqasm3}.
    Notably, the compiler allows fine-grained gate selection to match native hardware capabilities—for example, using Pauli gates on superconducting hardware or rotation gates on trapped-ion devices—facilitating hardware-aware optimization and broad applicability.

    \section{Method}\label{sec:method}
    The proposed compiler, \texttt{qompiler}, decomposes user-defined Hamiltonians into executable quantum circuits through a multi-stage decomposition framework.
    As a recap, given an input Hamiltonian $\hat{H}$, the quantum dynamics are fully prescribed by the time-dependent evolution operator
    \begin{equation}
        \label{eq:hamiltonian}
        \hat{U}\left( t \right) = e^{-i\hat{H}t/\hbar}
    \end{equation}
    In Schrödinger's representation, for example, the system state is given as
    \begin{equation}
        \label{eq:sysstate}
        \left\| \psi\left( t \right) \right> = \hat{U}\left( t \right)\left\| \psi_{0} \right>
    \end{equation}
    where $\left\| \psi_{0} \right>$ is the initial ket of an arbitrary system state.
    Whereas, most observable operators remain constant unless they are explicitly time-dependent.
    Equivalently, the formulation of the dynamics in Heisenberg's representation are
    \begin{equation}
        \label{eq:heisenberg}
        \hat{A} \left( t \right) = \hat{U}^{\dagger} \left( t \right) \hat{A}_{0} \hat{U} \left( t \right)
    \end{equation}
    where $\hat{A}_{0}$ is the initial operator for an arbitrary observable.
    Whereas, the state kets remain constants.

    For computational purposes, we express the evolution operator as a unitary matrix \( U \) on a suitably chosen basis,
    which serves as the starting point for this paper.
    We divide the overall workflow into two stages: compilation and interpretation.
    During compilation, \texttt{Qompiler} takes \( U \) as input and decomposes it into gate-level circuits expressed
    using an intermediate language (IL) designed by us.

    The compilation process comprises several key steps, including two-level decomposition (2L), swap gate decomposition (CX),
    control gate decomposition (CTRL), Euler rotation decomposition (EA), and the Solovay-Kitaev approximation (SK)~\cite{nielsen2010q,dawson2005sk}.
    All decomposition processes are configurable, offering control over compilation granularity.

    Next are the optimization passes.
    These built-in processes reduce and consolidate redundant gates, overly fragmented circuits,
    and other non-optimal components of the code.
    Think of the decomposition processes as `push`, whereas these processes act as `pull`.
    Through the optimization processes, we `pull back` some of the decomposition actions.
    By combining the actions in both the `push` and `pull` processes, we aim to find an optimal compilation result
    that respects the native constraints of the targeted quantum hardware.

    The final compiled code (quantum circuit) is represented in a B-Tree data structure,
    which encodes gate-level lineage via parent-child relationships among tree nodes, thereby providing robust traceability
    and verifiability, which will be discussed in Section~\ref{sec:traceability-and-verifiability}.

    A key feature of \texttt{qompiler} is its configurable compilation granularity,
    allowing users to tailor the resulting circuit to the gate sets best suited for the target hardware.
    For instance, superconducting quantum hardware often implements Pauli gates ($X$, $Y$, $Z$) virtually,
    offering zero-error and zero-latency execution;
    in this case, the compiler can be configured to prioritize Pauli gates.
    Conversely, trapped-ion platforms such as IonQ natively support arbitrary single-qubit rotations,
    including $R_x(\phi)$, $R_y(\phi)$, and $R_z(\phi)$, and the compiler can be adapted accordingly to produce rotation-based circuits.
    This hardware-aware configurability enables efficient, optimized circuit generation across diverse quantum backends
    while maintaining traceability and cross-platform compatibility.

    Finally, the interpretation stage.
    The compiler’s intermediate language (IL) is platform-independent and can be interpreted and rendered
    into executable circuits for specific quantum platforms.
    The IL can also be translated into other quantum software standards, such as OpenQASM, for broader compatibility~\cite{openqasm2,openqasm3}.
    This stage is designed to be highly adaptable and open to new platforms.
    We have demonstrated interpretation with quantum computing platforms such as Qiskit and Cirq.
    Support for other platforms can be added easily with minimal programming effort.

    If the configuration specifies a granularity of \texttt{MULTI\_TARGET},
    the decomposition terminates with multi-target operators.
    Otherwise, the unitary operator is sequentially decomposed into two-level unitary operators,
    single-qubit gates, and control-pruned gates.
    A granularity probe of type \texttt{PRINCIPAL} is then performed to determine
    whether the target granularity has been reached.
    If so, the output consists of principal rotational operators.
    If not, the decomposition continues into either Clifford+T gate sets or user-defined universal gate sets.
    For ease of reference, the terminology used in this paper is summarized in Table~\ref{tab:terminology}.

    The resulting IL is represented as a B-Tree data structure and stored in \textit{.qco} files.
    In the B-Tree structure, parent nodes generally correspond to coarser-grained operators,
    while child nodes represent the decomposed operators whose product recovers the parent node, e.g.,
    \begin{equation}
        \label{eq:parent-child-relation}
        \hat{P} = \prod_{i=1}^{n} \hat{C}_{i}.
    \end{equation}
    In general, the nodes of a B-Tree are layered hierarchically.

    \section{Optimizers}\label{sec:optimization}
    \texttt{Qompiler} compiles the input unitary operator into a sequence of target quantum gates
    through successive decomposition processes, analogous to a digestion process.
    This often produces consecutive gates that annihilate one another or gates with smaller-than-desired granularity.
    To compensate for this, we included the optimization passes act as a synthesis process to simplify the output circuit,
    combining multiple operators into fewer, more efficient gates.

    The optimizer class follows the sliding window design.
    Within a certain window size, the optimizer considers the overall effect of the gates and looks for simpler representations.


    This push-and-pull design not only maximizes the efficiency of the resulting circuit
    but also provides precise control over gate granularity.
    Furthermore, our compiler design can easily accommodate custom optimizers.
    For example, if a particular gate type is more efficient on a specific platform,
    an optimizer can be added to the compiler to exploit this advantage.


    \section{Traceability and Verifiability}\label{sec:traceability-and-verifiability}

    One of the key features of the current implementation of \texttt{qompiler} is
    its built-in support for traceability and verifiability.
    When applying quantum computing to real-world problems, such as quantum chemistry,
    the size of the quantum circuit can become very large.
    A quantum program for simulating a simple molecule like LiH in the STO-3G basis
    may require approximately $1000$ gates~\cite{Hong_2022}.
    Simulation programs for moderately large molecules can consist of millions~\cite{poulin2014,google_science_2020}.
    Keeping track of all these gates and ensuring correctness is challenging.
    \texttt{Qompiler} is designed with traceability and verifiability in mind from the outset.
    We organize the compiled code as a B-Tree data structure in the intermediate language.
    Traceability is encoded in the parent-child relationships.
    Each gate in the final program can be traced back to the specific operation that produced it.
    The built-in optimizers applied in later stages of compilation also record tracing information for their operations
    and preserve the original lineage.

    Overall, debugging the quantum circuit is made easier in \texttt{qompiler}
    thanks to the traceability and verifiability integral to the design.

    \section{Limitations}\label{sec:limitations}
    The entire compiler project was implemented in Python with numerical libraries such as NumPy and SciPy.
    Consequently, the underlying numerical errors limit the precision of the compiled quantum circuit.
    However, when the Solovay--Kitaev approximation is employed,
    the dominant source of error is the residual approximation error itself.

    As the size of the input increases, practical system limitations arise.
    The program becomes computationally intensive for large unitary matrices.
    The memory footprint may also become a bottleneck once the number of qubits reaches a certain threshold.
    It is expected that the pressure on computational resources can be alleviated through distributed computation.


    \section{Conclusion and Future Work}\label{sec:conclusion-and-future-work}
    We presented a configurable, cross-platform quantum compiler, \texttt{Qompiler}, with built-in gate traceability.
    Initial benchmarks confirm the correctness and consistency of \texttt{Qompiler}'s
    output across supported platforms, including Qiskit and Cirq.
    Future work will focus on distributed computation and parallelization to enable the
    compilation of large input matrices and to improve overall efficiency.
    We also plan to extend compatibility to additional frameworks, such as AWS Braket and PennyLane,
    with the broader goal of streamlining quantum algorithm development across the heterogeneous quantum
    computing landscape~\cite{braket-zenodo,pennylane}.
    In addition, we aim to incorporate quantum error correction techniques to enhance the reliability of synthesized circuits.
    We welcome collaboration and feedback from the quantum software community.
