When my doctoral research came to a complete stall after $6$ years of hard work, I was convinced
that I had no future as an unachieving physicist.
Digging into my old dreams, I was delighted to find that my passion for coding,
even though dormant throughout the years, is still burning.
Through a prolonged, self-taught learning, I incrementally acquired higher and higher proficiency
in software engineering skills.
From a mere passionate dilettante to an experienced professional it took me about a decade.
I struggled for the past ten years to get to the bottom of everything that bothers me.
This book is a summary of the struggles, the gains, the dark quests, and the `aha' moments
over the years.

I spent hours and hours of concentrated time to understand the mechanisms behind computer programs,
feel the relationships among various technologies, and draw multidisciplinary analogies
across seemingly different fields.

I try to reconcile new learning with what I already grasped.
Have you ever wondered why comparison-based sorting algorithms are unanimously bound by $O(N
\log N)$?
I managed to gain a personal, psychologically satisfying understanding through long time
contemplations.
Starting with specific sorting algorithms,
I related sorting operations as pumping Shannon's information entropy out of the subject of sorting,
just like pumping heat out of the room with AC\cite{Shannon-mathematical-1948}.
From there further up, I track it all the way back to the field of statistical
mechanics which was first developed by Ludwig Boltzmann in the 19th century\cite{landau:1980}.
Finally I ended up with modeling the time complexity of sorting algorithms by the statistical
entropy of an isolated system with $N$ particles.
This result is both surprising and expected to me.

My journey of learning and practicing in the field of computer sciences was materialized in
thousands of pages of Evernotes, $40k$
lines of code solving problems that I found online or offline, \textit{e.g.},
\url{https://projecteuler.net}, \url{https://leetcode.com}, and $100k+$ lines of
production code at top-tier tech companies.
Many of the problems that I worked on were revisited for four to five times over a period of
several years.
But each time I was able to gain new understandings regarding why my code worked or
not worked.
As a result my solution becomes shorter and cleaner over time.
I thought about sharing the stumbles, the struggles, the lessons, and the learning in a book.

But I didn't take this seriously until fairly recently when
I came across a website posting the research report by Forrester where
it cites that the prolonged software engineering talent shortage has turned from lack-of-quantity
to lack-of-quality \footnote{\url{https://go.forrester.com/research/predictions}}.
Then I realized that the data given in this report seems corroborated by my
observation.

As I progressed toward more and more mature a developer and starting about $5$ years ago,
I started to note that many other developers, colleagues of mine included,
struggled with the same problems that I used to struggle with.
I also realized that company-owned proprietary codebases that I happened to work with,
as well as open source ones, such as on Github\footnote{\url{github.com}}, are filled with
copy-n-pasted code that could have been gotten rid of with a shared library of static functions,
intricate logic that could have been simplified and achieved by a one-liner,
sneaky bugs due to contamination by variables escaped its due scope,
monolithic, all-inclusive mammoth that could have been refactored into
smaller, single-purposed functions, and so on.
Many famous projects which we thought should be highly dependable are no exception.
It is unbelievable that our daily life, our depended-upon transactions, relies on code that
`smells'.
Beside getters, setters and other boilerplate code, implementations of fairly dense algorithm,
designs of interrelated classes and methods, are almost guaranteed to contain one or more of the
numerous `smells' as listed
in Martin Fowler's seminal book `Refactor'~\cite{Fowler1999}.
In fact I seldom see succinct code as examples given in `Programming Pearls'
and others of its sort\cite{Bentley:1986:PP}.
Ugly code becomes the norm and beautiful code the exception.
and convince yourself that many of these great project came with a great design but were
implemented with mediocrity.

Personally, I've worked with graduates and post-graduates from top-tier schools
during design reviews, code reviews, and technical interviews.
They come with latest theories in computer sciences.
Their resumes are full of buzz words.
They have eye-catching GPA\@.
They even spent a great deal of time with books on principles, patterns, guidance,
and advice of software development.
But at the end of the day when it's time to code up ideas, they clearly needed help with
how to practice them in industry settings.
These plain facts convey the crying signal that there is a gap between the domain of best practices
and the domain how to practice the best practices.

\section{Who should read this book?} \label{sec:whoshouldread}

I hope readers would have the same reaction to many of the topics in this book
as Jon Bentley put in `Programming Pearls'\cite{Bentley:1986:PP}, namely, ``This sure looks
familiar'' followed with ``I've never seen this before.''

Yes,
this book is intended for professionals who have already had a solid command on computer science
foundations, but they still find that applying them in kaleidoscopic coding situations
remains challenging nonetheless.


It assumes readers have familiarity with the popular programming languages such as \python, \java,
C++ \textit{etc.}.
It is assumed that reader have a substantial understanding in basic algorithms, data structures,
and operating systems as these are not the focus of this book, at least not systematically.
Where needed, an algorithm may be explained in detail for the sake of implementation.

\section{What this book is not about}\label{sec:thisBookIsNotAbout}

Some disclaimers are due here.

Firstly this book is \emph{not} a systematic programming primer.
Rather than focusing on complete coverage of most programming aspects,
it comprises of a scarce of organic programming experiences, home-made software
development techniques, and other observed patterns that are based on years of personal immersion
in the software industry and many of them are not talked about elsewhere.

Also this book is not squarely focused on interview problem-solving skills.
Problem-solving in a interview settings often emphasizes too much on `getting the job done'.
In a limited time, one is expected to understand and analyze the requirement, brainstorming for
possible solutions, design, algorithm picking and tailoring, data structure fitting, \textit{etc.}
Getting the job done in such a setting is at most biased if used solely to identify the right
candidate.

It inevitably fails to evaluate candidate for the skill set truly needed at work
such as code refactor and reuse.

Instead, interview setting should be as close to real work environment as possible.

Through an interview, we want to be sure that candidates can or have the potential to produce
usable, reusable, and readable code for large scale production.

The focus of this writing is to help beginning and intermediate developers to advance their
coding skills to next level.
The techniques discussed here are practical in production coding environment.
But they are not necessarily helpful during interview settings.
Therefore this book may be an overkill for interview prep.

I am not proposing new rules or new principles.
Many of the principles, programming patterns already exists.
What I attempt to do here is to show you how to turn these principles to correct and elegant code.
My ways are not to be accepted but to be felt and verified in your own practice.

Other than that, this book does not teach you any specific programming language.
It assumes readers have familiarity with the popular programming languages such as \python, \java,
C++ \textit{etc.}.
Neither does this book focus on algorithms or data structures, at least not systematically.
Where needed, the workings of an algorithms are outlined for reader's convenience.
That should be taken as the focus of the book.

This book is not about recipe or cookbook for any specific framework or system.
Rather, it's a general guideline for development with all frameworks and systems.

Last but not least, this book is not just about technical interviews.
Those who are preparing for technical interviews will benefit by reading this book.
But the main target of this book are those who are determined for long-term investment
of improving their programming skills.
Nevertheless, here and there, I use interview problems to illustrate the strategy of
implementation of certain algorithms.
But neither coding questions nor their solutions are the focus of this book.
The strategy of implementing functions and classes is not only applicable to coding questions
for an interview, but more so for software development at work.

Last but not least, it takes years of combat training in real battle fields to achieve a higher
level of proficiency.
It is, therefore, not my pursuance to help you cut short the path.

Finally, I hope the reader would take this book as a seed rather than food, as an inspiration for
endeavors rather than sharing of achievement for applause.
